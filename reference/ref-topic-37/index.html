Saw Blade Types

Circular Saw and Table Saw Blades

Saw blade selection is defined by tooth geometry, tooth count, kerf width, and blade material. The two fundamental tooth grinds are Flat Top Grind (FTG) for ripping wood along the grain, and Alternate Top Bevel (ATB) for crosscutting across the grain. FTG teeth have a flat cutting edge that acts like a chisel, efficiently slicing through wood fibers lengthwise but tearing out surface fibers on crosscuts. ATB teeth have alternating beveled edges (typically 10-20 degrees) that slice cleanly through wood fibers on both sides of the cut, producing a smooth crosscut finish with minimal tear-out. A Combination (Comb) or ATBR blade alternates groups of four ATB teeth with one FTG tooth, providing a compromise between ripping and crosscutting performance in a single general-purpose blade. Triple Chip Grind (TCG) teeth alternate between a flat tooth and a chamfered tooth; this profile excels at cutting abrasive materials like laminates, MDF, and non-ferrous metals (aluminum, brass). Dado blades are stacked sets of two outer blades with interleaving chippers that create flat-bottomed grooves and dadoes from 1/4 to 29/32 inch wide in a single pass on a table saw and are essential for joinery. Tooth count determines cut quality and feed rate: a 24-tooth blade is aggressive and fast for ripping framing lumber; a 40-tooth combination blade is the best all-around choice for general woodworking; a 60-tooth ATB blade produces smooth crosscuts in plywood and trim; and an 80-tooth fine-finish blade produces nearly polished cuts in melamine, veneered plywood, and plastic laminates with minimal chipping. Thin-kerf blades (about 0.090-0.098 inch kerf) require less motor power and produce less waste, ideal for jobsite saws and battery-powered tools. Full-kerf blades (0.125-0.130 inch) are stiffer and produce more accurate cuts in hardwoods, preferred for cabinet saws with 3+ HP motors.

Blade Anatomy and Hook Angle

The hook angle (rake angle) of the teeth dramatically affects cutting behavior. A positive hook angle (15-20 degrees) causes the tooth to aggressively dig into the material, which is desirable for ripping softwood and fast feed rates but dangerous for sliding miter saws cutting thick stock (self-feeding can cause climb-cutting). A neutral or negative hook angle (0 to -5 degrees) requires more forward pressure but is much safer for radial arm saws, miter saws, and cutting non-ferrous metals because it prevents the blade from grabbing and climbing. Carbide-tipped teeth are brazed onto hardened steel blade bodies and can be sharpened multiple times. Laser-cut expansion slots in the blade body reduce noise, dissipate heat, and prevent warping. Vibration-dampening plates (typically copper-plug-filled laser cuts) reduce resonance in thin-kerf blades. A properly tuned saw should produce a clean cut with minimal burning or feathering; burn marks indicate a dull blade, misaligned fence, or too slow a feed rate.

  • Positive hook (15-20 deg): aggressive rip, table saw
  • Negative hook (0 to -5 deg): miter saw, radial arm, metal cutting
  • Thin kerf: ~0.090 in, less waste, lower power requirement
  • Full kerf: ~0.125 in, stiffer, better for hardwoods
  • Carbide tips: last 10-50x longer than steel teeth
  • Blade diameter: 7-1/4 in (circular saw), 10 in (table/miter saw), 12 in (miter saw)
