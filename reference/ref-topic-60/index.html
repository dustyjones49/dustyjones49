Adhesive Types Guide

Choosing the Right Adhesive

Adhesives have largely replaced mechanical fasteners for many construction applications, offering benefits such as distributed load transfer, elimination of thermal bridging, and the ability to bond dissimilar materials. Selecting the correct adhesive requires matching the chemistry to the substrate materials, environmental conditions, and structural requirements. Using the wrong adhesive can result in bond failure, which in load-bearing applications can have serious consequences.

Construction adhesive is a heavy-duty, gap-filling adhesive formulated for bonding building materials including subflooring, drywall, paneling, and foam board. Typically based on polyurethane or modified silicone chemistry, construction adhesives maintain flexibility after curing, accommodating the natural expansion and contraction of building materials. They are applied with a caulking gun in a zigzag bead pattern. Wood glue, specifically polyvinyl acetate or aliphatic resin, creates a bond stronger than the wood itself when properly clamped. The glue penetrates wood fibers and locks them together as it cures. It is not waterproof unless specifically labeled as Type II or Type III water-resistant, making it unsuitable for exterior applications.

Epoxy adhesives are two-component systems consisting of resin and hardener. Once mixed, they cure through a chemical reaction to form an extremely strong, waterproof bond that fills gaps and bonds to nearly any material: metal, glass, ceramics, most plastics, and wood. Epoxy is the go-to adhesive for structural repairs where maximum strength is required. Cyanoacrylate, commonly called super glue, bonds instantly to skin and many non-porous surfaces through a reaction with surface moisture. It has very high tensile strength but low shear and impact strength, making it ideal for small, precise repairs but poor for load-bearing joints. Polyurethane adhesive like Gorilla Glue expands as it cures, filling gaps and creating a strong bond on wood, stone, metal, and many plastics. Contact cement bonds on contact and is used primarily for laminating plastic laminate to countertop substrates.
