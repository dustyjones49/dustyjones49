Fastener Head Types

Screw Head Selection

The head of a screw serves multiple functions: it provides the driving surface, distributes clamping load, and determines whether the fastener sits proud, flush, or countersunk. Selecting the wrong head type can lead to stripped drivers, inadequate clamping force, or a finished appearance that requires additional trim work to conceal. Understanding head geometry and its relationship to driver type is fundamental to efficient fastening.

Flat head screws have an 82- or 90-degree conical underside that allows them to sit flush with or below the surface when driven into a countersunk hole. This is the standard head for wood screws and drywall screws where a flush finish is required. Flat heads are driven with Phillips, square, or Torx bits. Pan head screws have a flat bearing surface on the underside with a slightly domed top, providing greater clamping area than flat heads. They sit above the surface and are commonly used in metal-to-metal connections, electrical boxes, and applications where countersinking is not practical. Round head screws have a fully domed top with a flat underside, offering the largest bearing surface of any head type.

Hex head fasteners, also called hex cap screws, have a six-sided head driven with a socket or wrench. The large drive surface allows for high torque application without stripping, making hex heads the standard for structural bolting, lag screws, and machinery mounting. Bugle head screws have a curved transition from shank to head rather than a sharp cone, designed specifically for drywall installation. The bugle shape allows the screw to dimple the drywall paper without tearing it, creating a depression that is easily filled with joint compound. Trim head screws have an especially small diameter head with a minimal profile, allowing them to be countersunk and concealed with wood filler for finish carpentry applications where the fastener should be nearly invisible.
