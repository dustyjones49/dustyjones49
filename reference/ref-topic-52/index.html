Understanding Concrete Strength Gain

Concrete does not dry to gain strength; it cures through a chemical reaction called hydration. Cement particles react with water to form calcium silicate hydrate, the binding agent that gives concrete its compressive strength. This process continues for years, but the most significant strength gain occurs within the first 28 days. The industry standard design strength, typically 3,000 to 5,000 PSI for residential work, is measured at 28 days under controlled laboratory conditions.

At day 1 after placement, concrete typically achieves only 16% of its 28-day design strength. By day 3, it reaches approximately 40%. The 7-day mark is a critical milestone, with concrete reaching about 60-70% of design strength. At this point, most formwork can be removed and light construction traffic may be allowed. At 14 days, strength reaches roughly 80-90%. The full 100% design strength is achieved at 28 days, which is when engineers base their structural calculations. Concrete continues to gain strength beyond 28 days: at 90 days, it may reach 110-120% of the 28-day value, and after one year, some mixes reach 130%.

Temperature dramatically affects curing rates. Concrete cured at 73 degrees Fahrenheit follows the standard curve. At 90 degrees, early strength gain accelerates significantly but ultimate strength may be slightly lower. Below 50 degrees, hydration slows dramatically, and below 40 degrees, special cold-weather concreting measures are required, such as heated enclosures and accelerators. Proper moisture curing is equally important: concrete kept continuously wet for 7 days can achieve 50% higher strength than concrete cured in dry air. Wet burlap, plastic sheeting, or curing compounds should be applied immediately after finishing.
