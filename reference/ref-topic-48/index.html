Window Efficiency Ratings

NFRC Performance Metrics

The National Fenestration Rating Council (NFRC) label is the industry standard for comparing window energy performance. Every certified window carries an NFRC label with four primary metrics measured under standardized laboratory conditions. U-Factor measures the rate of conductive heat transfer through the entire window assembly (frame, glass, and edge spacer) in units of BTU per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit. A lower U-factor means better insulation. Single-pane windows have a U-factor of about 1.0; double-pane clear glass is around 0.40 to 0.50; double-pane with Low-E coating and argon gas fill achieves 0.27 to 0.30; and triple-pane windows can reach 0.15 to 0.20. The appropriate U-factor for a given home depends on the climate zone, with colder zones requiring lower U-factors. Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC) measures the fraction of solar radiation admitted through the window, expressed as a number between 0 and 1. A higher SHGC means the window transmits more solar heat. In cold climates, a higher SHGC (0.40 to 0.55) is desirable to capture free passive solar heat. In hot climates, a low SHGC (0.25 or lower) is needed to reduce cooling loads. Visible Transmittance (VT) measures the amount of visible light that passes through the window, also on a 0-to-1 scale. A higher VT means more daylight enters the room, reducing the need for electric lighting. Most clear-glass windows have a VT between 0.50 and 0.70. Low-E coatings can be spectrally selective — allowing high VT while maintaining low SHGC — which is the ideal combination for mixed and cooling-dominated climates. The Light-to-Solar Gain (LSG) ratio = VT / SHGC. An LSG greater than 1.25 indicates a spectrally selective glazing that transmits more visible light than solar heat. Air Leakage (AL) measures the rate at which air passes through the window assembly (in cubic feet per minute per square foot of window). The NFRC maximum allowable AL is 0.3 cfm/sq ft, but quality windows achieve 0.1 or lower. Lower AL means less draft and less infiltration heat loss. Condensation Resistance (CR) is an optional rating from 1 to 100; a higher CR number indicates better resistance to interior surface condensation, which is a function of glass temperature (driven by U-factor) and frame design.

Energy Star Window Requirements by Climate Zone

Energy Star certifies windows, doors, and skylights based on performance criteria tailored to the climate zone where the product will be installed. The U.S. is divided into four Energy Star Climate Zones: Northern (mostly heating-dominated), North-Central, South-Central, and Southern (mostly cooling-dominated). For the Northern zone (roughly north of the 45th parallel), Energy Star version 7.0 (effective October 2023) requires a maximum U-factor of 0.22 and allows any SHGC. For the North-Central zone, U-factor must be 0.25 max with any SHGC. For the South-Central zone, U-factor must be 0.28 max and SHGC must be 0.23 max. For the Southern zone, U-factor must be 0.32 max and SHGC must be 0.23 max. These values are the minimum required for the Energy Star label; windows exceeding these minimums qualify as "Most Efficient" Energy Star designation.

  • Northern zone: U ≤ 0.22, any SHGC
  • North-Central: U ≤ 0.25, any SHGC
  • South-Central: U ≤ 0.28, SHGC ≤ 0.23
  • Southern: U ≤ 0.32, SHGC ≤ 0.23
  • Triple-pane: U 0.15-0.20, best for cold climates
  • Low-E coating: types of emissivity coatings (hard coat vs soft coat, single vs dual vs triple silver layers)
