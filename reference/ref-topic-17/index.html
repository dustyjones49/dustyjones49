Nail Size Chart

Nail Sizes from 2d to 60d

Nail sizes are traditionally denoted by the "penny" system, abbreviated with the letter "d" (from the Roman denarius). The penny size originated in England centuries ago and indicated the price per hundred nails of that size; today it corresponds to a specific length. A 2d nail is 1 inch long, and sizes increase roughly in quarter-inch increments until 10d, after which the increments vary. Common nails have a thicker shank and larger flat head, making them the go-to choice for rough framing and general construction. Box nails have a thinner shank than common nails of the same penny size, reducing splitting in thinner wood. Sinker nails (coated) have a slightly thinner shank with a textured or resin coating for enhanced holding power and easier driving. Finishing nails have small, barrel-shaped heads that can be set below the wood surface with a nail set and covered with wood filler. Casing nails are similar to finishing nails but slightly thicker and with a conical head. Roofing nails have an extra-large flat head (3/8 to 7/16 inch diameter) to hold shingles without tearing. Duplex nails (scaffold nails) have two heads — the lower head is driven flush and the upper head remains exposed for easy removal during temporary construction.

Nail Selection by Application

Building codes specify nail type, size, and spacing for structural connections. The IRC requires 8d common nails at 6-inch edges and 12-inch field spacing for roof and wall sheathing. Floor sheathing requires 8d common or deformed nails at 6-inch edges and 12-inch field, or approved ring-shank nails. For framing connections, 16d common nails are standard for stud-to-plate connections (3 nails per stud end). Sinkers, while popular for speed, have 10-15% less withdrawal resistance than common nails of the same penny size, so when a code calls for "16d common," a 16d sinker is not a compliant substitute. Galvanized nails are required for pressure-treated lumber because the copper-based preservatives are corrosive to standard steel. Hot-dipped galvanized offers far better corrosion resistance than electro-galvanized. For structural shear walls, common 8d or 10d nails at specified edge distances hold the OSB or plywood to the framing.

  • Sheathing: 8d common, 6 in edges, 12 in field (IRC Table R602.3)
  • Floor sheathing: 8d common or deformed, 6 in edges, 12 in field
  • Framing: 16d common, 3 per stud-to-plate connection
  • Pressure-treated: hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel
  • Withdrawal resistance: common > sinker > box nail
  • Ring-shank: ~200% more withdrawal resistance than smooth
