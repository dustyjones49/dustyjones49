Conducting an Energy Audit

A home energy audit identifies where a house is losing energy and which improvements will deliver the greatest return on investment. Professional audits using blower door tests and infrared cameras provide the most accurate assessment, but homeowners can perform a basic walk-through audit to identify obvious problems. The Department of Energy estimates that sealing air leaks and adding insulation can reduce heating and cooling costs by up to 20 percent, with the audit itself often paying for itself within the first year of implementing recommendations.

Air sealing is typically the most cost-effective energy improvement. Common leak locations include the attic floor penetrations for plumbing vents, electrical wiring, recessed lights, and chimney chases. The basement rim joist area where the foundation meets the wood framing is another major leakage zone. Weatherstripping around doors and windows deteriorates over time and should be checked annually using the dollar-bill test: close the door or window on a dollar bill and if it pulls out easily, the seal needs replacing. Exterior wall penetrations for hose bibs, dryer vents, and cable entries should be sealed with caulk or spray foam. The cumulative effect of sealing small leaks throughout the house can equal leaving a window open year-round.

Insulation levels should be checked against Department of Energy recommendations for the climate zone. Attic insulation is the most important: DOE recommends R-38 to R-60 in most climate zones, equivalent to 10 to 16 inches of fiberglass batt or blown-in insulation. Wall insulation is more difficult to verify in existing homes but can be checked by removing an outlet cover and probing the wall cavity. Floor insulation over unconditioned spaces should meet R-25 to R-30. HVAC system efficiency is rated by SEER for air conditioners and AFUE for furnaces. Systems older than 15 years should be evaluated for replacement with ENERGY STAR certified equipment. Duct leakage, particularly in unconditioned attics and crawl spaces, can waste 20 to 30 percent of conditioned air. Duct sealing with mastic and duct insulation are cost-effective improvements. Lighting accounts for approximately 10 percent of residential electricity use; replacing incandescent bulbs with LEDs reduces lighting energy use by 75 to 80 percent.
