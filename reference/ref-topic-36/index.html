Sandpaper Grit Guide

Grit Ratings and Sanding Progression

Sandpaper grit numbers refer to the number of abrasive particles per square inch of the backing material — lower numbers mean fewer, larger particles (coarse), and higher numbers mean more, smaller particles (fine). The scale is divided into coarse (24-60 grit), medium (80-120 grit), fine (150-180 grit), very fine (220-320 grit), and extra fine/micro-abrasive (360 grit and above). A proper sanding progression never skips more than one grit level. Jumping from 80 grit directly to 220 grit leaves deep scratches that will telegraph through the finish. The standard progression for bare wood preparation for stain/finish is 80-120-180 (or 80-120-220). For paint removal or aggressive stock removal, start at 40 or 60 grit. For sanding between coats of finish, use 220-320 grit applied very lightly. For polishing film finishes to a high gloss, wet sanding with 400, 600, 1200, and even 2000-3000 grit is used followed by rubbing and polishing compounds. Aluminum oxide is the most common abrasive for woodworking sands because the particles self-sharpen as they fracture. Silicon carbide (wet-dry paper) is harder and sharper, used for wet sanding finishes, metal, and automotive applications. Garnet abrasive (typically reddish-colored paper) is natural and less aggressive, producing a smooth surface for fine woodworking but wearing faster than synthetic abrasives. Ceramic/alumina-zirconia blends are used in coarse grits for aggressive stock removal on belt sanders. When hand-sanding, always sand with the grain direction, not across it. Random orbital sanders use a circular/elliptical motion that does not follow the grain, but they leave inconspicuous swirl marks when used through the full progression to at least 180 grit. For varnish and polyurethane topcoats, wet sanding between coats with 400-grit silicon carbide on a hard backing block eliminates dust nibs and promotes adhesion of subsequent coats without cutting through the film.

Abrasive Types and Backing

The abrasive material and backing determine sandpaper performance and longevity. Paper backing (A-weight through F-weight) is rated by weight and flexibility. A-weight is light and flexible for hand sanding contoured surfaces. C-weight is the standard for most sheet sandpapers. D-weight is heavy for belt sander belts. Cloth backing (J-weight for flexible, X-weight for heavy duty) is used for belts and floor sanding rolls where high tensile strength is needed. Film-backed abrasives are used for precision applications where a uniform, durable surface is essential. Hook-and-loop (Velcro) backing is standard for random orbital sanders in 5-inch and 6-inch diameters with dust extraction holes that must align with the pad. PSA (pressure-sensitive adhesive) discs stick directly to the pad and are used for detail sanders and some DA sanders. Stearated ("no-load" or "non-clogging") sandpapers have a zinc stearate coating that prevents dust and resin from clogging the abrasive, extending paper life in wood sanding.

  • Aluminum oxide: all-purpose wood sanding (self-sharpening)
  • Silicon carbide: wet/dry, metal, auto body (hardest common abrasive)
  • Garnet: natural, fine woodworking, slower cutting
  • Ceramic (alumina-zirconia): coarse grinding, heavy stock removal
  • Sanding progression: never skip more than 1 grit (80-120-180, not 80-220)
  • Random orbital: go to minimum 180 grit before finishing
