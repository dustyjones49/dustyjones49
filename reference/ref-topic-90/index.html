Identifying Asbestos in Buildings

Asbestos was used extensively in building materials from the 1920s through the 1970s for its fire resistance, insulation value, tensile strength, and affordability. While most asbestos-containing products were banned from new use in the United States by the late 1980s, millions of homes and commercial buildings constructed before that date still contain asbestos materials. Disturbing these materials during renovation or demolition releases microscopic fibers that, when inhaled, cause mesothelioma, asbestosis, and lung cancer, typically decades after exposure.

Floor tile installed between 1920 and 1980 commonly contains asbestos, particularly 9-inch by 9-inch vinyl tiles, which were almost always manufactured with asbestos fiber reinforcement. The mastic adhesive used to install these tiles, typically black cutback adhesive, also frequently contains asbestos. Sheet vinyl flooring with a fibrous backing installed before 1980 is another common source. Pipe insulation is perhaps the most recognizable asbestos material: a white or gray corrugated cardboard-like wrapping on steam pipes and boiler fittings in older basements. This "airstream" insulation is friable, meaning it easily crumbles and releases fibers, making it one of the most hazardous forms of asbestos in buildings.

Popcorn ceilings, also called acoustic or textured ceilings, installed between 1945 and 1980 frequently contain 1 to 10 percent chrysotile asbestos mixed into the spray-applied texture. Popcorn ceiling removal is especially hazardous because the material is friable and the overhead work position increases inhalation risk. Asbestos cement siding, manufactured as rigid shingles and panels from the 1920s through the 1980s, is less hazardous than friable forms because the fibers are locked within the cement matrix. However, cutting, grinding, or breaking these materials releases fibers. Roofing materials including asphalt shingles, roofing felt, and flashing compounds manufactured before 1980 may contain asbestos. Vermiculite attic insulation, particularly product sourced from the Libby, Montana mine, is contaminated with tremolite asbestos and represents a significant hazard if disturbed.
