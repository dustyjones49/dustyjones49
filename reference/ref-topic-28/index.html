Insulation R-Value by Climate Zone

DOE Climate Zone R-Value Requirements

The Department of Energy divides the United States into eight climate zones based on heating degree days (HDD) and cooling degree days (CDD), each with prescribed minimum insulation R-values for new residential construction. Zone 1 (southern Florida and Hawaii, very hot-humid) requires the lowest insulation levels: R-13 for walls and R-30 for attics. Zone 2 (Gulf Coast through southern Arizona, hot-humid or hot-dry) needs R-13 walls and R-38 attics. Zones 3 and 4, covering large portions of the Southeast, Mid-Atlantic, and Pacific Northwest, require R-13 to R-20 walls and R-38 to R-49 attics. Zone 5 (Midwest, lower Northeast, inland Northwest) calls for R-20 cavity or R-13+5 continuous wall insulation and R-49 attics. Zone 6 (upper Midwest, upper Northeast, mountain West) requires R-20+5 cavity or R-13+10 continuous and R-49 attics. Zone 7 (northern Minnesota, North Dakota, high mountains) goes to R-20+5 or R-13+10 walls and R-49 attics. Zone 8 (Arctic, northern Alaska) requires the highest levels: R-20+10 or R-13+15 walls and R-60 attics. These values are minimums per the 2021 International Energy Conservation Code (IECC); many builders exceed them for better performance and lower energy bills. The R-value of a material measures its resistance to conductive heat flow — the higher the R-value, the better the insulating power. The total assembly R-value accounts for the insulation plus framing, sheathing, drywall, and air films.

Insulation Types and Effective R-Values

Different insulation materials provide different R-values per inch of thickness. Fiberglass batts provide R-2.9 to R-3.8 per inch depending on density (standard vs. high-performance). Blown cellulose is roughly R-3.5 per inch. Closed-cell spray foam is the highest at R-6.0 to R-7.0 per inch and acts as an air and vapor barrier. Open-cell spray foam is about R-3.6 per inch and is an air barrier but not a vapor barrier. Rigid foam boards range from R-3.6 per inch (EPS) to R-5.0 per inch (polyisocyanurate) to R-5.0 per inch (XPS). Continuous exterior insulation (ci) is critical in cold climates because it eliminates thermal bridging through wall studs, which can reduce the whole-wall R-value by 15-25% compared to cavity-only R-value. Window U-factor and SHGC requirements also vary by climate zone.
