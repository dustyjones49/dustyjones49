Drill Bit Types

Drill Bit Types by Application

Twist drill bits are the universal general-purpose bit used for drilling holes in wood, plastic, and light-gauge metal. They are identified by their 118-degree standard point angle (or 135-degree split-point for self-centering), two cutting edges, and helical flutes that evacuate chips. Standard twist bits are inexpensive, widely available in fractional, numbered, and lettered size sets, and perform adequately in most materials when used with proper speed and feed pressure. Brad-point bits have a sharp center spur that prevents wandering and two outer spurs that score the hole perimeter for an ultra-clean entry hole in wood, making them the go-to choice for furniture-grade joinery and dowel holes. Spade bits (paddle bits) have a wide flat blade with a center point and two cutting edges, boring large-diameter holes (1/4 to 1-1/2 inch) quickly through framing lumber for electrical and plumbing rough-ins. They are rough-cutting tools that leave a splintered exit hole. Forstner bits produce flat-bottomed, clean-sided holes with no center spur breakthrough in wood, ideal for concealed hinges (European-style cup hinges), pocket holes, and overlapping holes. Hole saws are cylindrical saw blades with a pilot drill in the center, used for cutting large-diameter holes (3/4 to 6 inches) for door lock sets, pipe penetrations, and recessed lighting. Masonry bits have a carbide-tipped wedge-shaped cutting edge and are used exclusively in rotary hammer mode (not standard drill mode) in a hammer drill to drill into brick, concrete block, and poured concrete. Step bits (unibit) are cone-shaped bits with progressively larger cutting steps, ideal for drilling clean holes in thin sheet metal, plastic panels, and electrical junction boxes. Auger bits have a screw-tip lead that pulls the bit into wood, a wide flute for fast chip evacuation, and are used for drilling deep framing holes in timber construction and timber framing.

Drilling Speed and Technique

Proper drilling speed depends on bit diameter and material. As a general rule, smaller bits require higher RPM and larger bits require lower RPM. In metal, lubricating cutting oil (or even WD-40 for aluminum) must be used to dissipate heat and prevent work hardening the metal. In wood, backing up the workpiece with a sacrificial board prevents blowout on the exit side. For large-diameter holes with spade bits or hole saws, drilling from both sides (drill until the pilot point emerges, then finish from the opposite face) produces a clean hole on both faces. Masonry bits require a hammer drill with rotary hammer action — the hammering pulses break the material while the rotation clears dust from the flutes. Never use a masonry bit in standard rotation-only mode; it will overheat, dull the carbide, and take forever to drill.

  • Standard twist point angle: 118 deg (general), 135 deg split-point (self-centering)
  • Brad-point: for furniture-grade clean entry/exit in wood
  • Forstner: flat bottom, no breakthrough spur
  • Hole saw: use low speed, back off frequently to clear teeth
  • Masonry: use hammer drill only (SDS-plus or SDS-max for larger bits)
  • Metal drilling: use cutting oil, lower RPM, steady pressure
