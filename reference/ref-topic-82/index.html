Design Wind Speed Zones

Wind loads are a primary design consideration for building structures, particularly roofs, exterior walls, and fenestrations. The American Society of Civil Engineers standard ASCE 7 defines design wind speeds based on 3-second gust measurements at 33 feet above ground in open terrain, with a mean recurrence interval of 700 years for most Risk Category II buildings. These speeds are mapped by county and inform structural design requirements including connection strengths, roof sheathing nailing patterns, and window and door impact resistance ratings.

The lowest design wind speeds in the United States, typically 90 to 105 mph, are found in the interior regions of the country shielded from both hurricane and tornado activity. These areas include the upper Midwest, Great Plains, and interior mountain West. Buildings in these zones require standard wind-resistant construction including properly nailed roof sheathing, hurricane clips at roof-to-wall connections, and anchor bolts at sill plates. Moderate wind zones of 110 to 130 mph cover much of the Midwest, Northeast, and interior Southeast, where thunderstorms and frontal systems produce occasional high wind events.

High wind zones of 140 to 150 mph are concentrated along the Gulf Coast and Atlantic seaboard, where hurricanes make landfall. The highest design wind speeds in the contiguous United States are found in South Florida, particularly Miami-Dade and Broward counties, where the mapped wind speed exceeds 170 mph in some exposure categories. The hurricane-prone region, defined as areas within 100 miles of the Gulf and Atlantic coasts, requires impact-resistant glazing or shutter protection for all glazed openings. Wind-borne debris regions within one mile of the coast have even more stringent requirements. Special wind regions like mountain passes, canyons, and Alaska's coastal areas may exceed mapped values and require site-specific wind studies for design.
