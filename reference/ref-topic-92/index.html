Understanding Radon Risk

Radon is a colorless, odorless, radioactive gas produced by the natural decay of uranium in soil, rock, and groundwater. It enters buildings through cracks in foundations, gaps around pipes, and other openings in contact with the ground. Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking, responsible for approximately 21,000 deaths annually in the United States. The EPA has mapped the entire country into three radon potential zones to help target testing and mitigation resources.

EPA Zone 1 counties have a predicted average indoor radon screening level greater than 4 picocuries per liter, the EPA's recommended action level. Zone 1 covers large areas of the northern Great Plains, the Appalachian Mountains, the upper Midwest, and parts of the Northeast. Homes in Zone 1 counties should always be tested for radon, and radon-resistant new construction features, including a passive sub-slab depressurization system, should be installed in all new homes. Zone 2 counties have predicted average indoor radon levels between 2 and 4 pCi/L. Testing is strongly recommended but not universally required. Zone 3 counties have predicted levels below 2 pCi/L. While risk is lower, the EPA still recommends testing because high radon levels can occur in any geographic area due to localized geology.

Radon testing is simple and inexpensive. Short-term tests using charcoal canisters or electret ion chambers remain in place for 2 to 90 days. Long-term tests using alpha track detectors remain in place for 90 days to one year and provide a more accurate picture of year-round average exposure. If test results exceed 4 pCi/L, the EPA recommends mitigation. Active soil depressurization is the most common and effective mitigation method, using a fan to draw radon from beneath the foundation and vent it above the roofline. Mitigation systems typically cost $800 to $2,500 and can reduce indoor radon levels by up to 99 percent.
