Concrete Admixture Types

Overview of Concrete Admixtures

Concrete admixtures are materials added to the concrete mix before or during mixing to modify the properties of fresh or hardened concrete. These chemical compounds allow engineers and contractors to tailor concrete performance for specific project conditions, whether that means placing concrete in extreme temperatures, achieving higher early strengths, or improving workability without adding water. The proper selection and dosage of admixtures can make the difference between a successful pour and a costly failure.

Water-reducing admixtures are among the most commonly used. They allow a reduction in water content of 5% to 12% while maintaining the same slump, resulting in higher strength and lower permeability concrete. Mid-range water reducers can achieve 12% to 30% water reduction and are used where both workability and strength are priorities. Accelerating admixtures speed up the hydration reaction, making them invaluable for cold-weather concreting where low temperatures would otherwise cause dangerously slow strength gain. Calcium chloride is the most common accelerator, though non-chloride accelerators are preferred where embedded steel is present to avoid corrosion risk.

Retarding admixtures slow the setting time, which is essential for hot-weather concreting, long hauls from the batch plant, and large pours where cold joints must be avoided. Air-entraining admixtures create microscopic air bubbles that improve freeze-thaw resistance, critical for exterior concrete in northern climates. Superplasticizers, also called high-range water reducers, can reduce water content by 30% or more, producing flowing, self-leveling concrete without sacrificing strength. These are essential for congested reinforcement areas and architectural concrete requiring a smooth surface finish.
