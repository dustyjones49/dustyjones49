Easement Types

Understanding Property Easements

An easement is a legal right allowing a person or entity to use another person's land for a specific purpose. Easements are recorded on the property deed and run with the land, meaning they remain in effect when the property is sold. For builders and homeowners, easements impose restrictions on where structures can be placed and what activities can occur within the easement area. Violating an easement by building within it can result in a court order to remove the encroaching structure at the property owner's expense.

Utility easements are the most common type affecting residential properties. They grant utility companies the right to install, maintain, and access infrastructure including power lines, gas mains, water pipes, sewer lines, and telecommunications cables. Utility easements typically run along front property lines with widths of 5 to 15 feet or along rear lot lines. Drainage easements are established to manage stormwater runoff across properties. They prohibit structures and grading changes that would obstruct the designed drainage path. Drainage easements are common in subdivisions with detention basins, swales, and culverts, and are critical for preventing flooding of adjacent properties.

Access easements provide a right of way to reach a landlocked property or shared resource. Driveway easements allow a neighbor to cross your property to access their home, while private road easements grant shared use of a roadway serving multiple properties. Beach and lake access easements are common in waterfront communities. Conservation easements restrict development to protect natural resources, wildlife habitat, scenic views, or agricultural land. These easements are held by land trusts or government agencies and permanently limit building, subdivision, and certain land uses in exchange for tax benefits. As a property owner, you retain ownership and may continue traditional uses like farming, but future development rights are permanently extinguished.
