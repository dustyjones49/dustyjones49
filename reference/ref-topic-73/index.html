Window Treatment Types

Selecting Window Treatments

Window treatments serve four primary functions: controlling natural light, providing privacy, insulating against heat loss and gain, and contributing to the room's aesthetic. The right treatment balances these priorities based on the window's orientation, the room's function, and the local climate. A south-facing window in a hot climate demands different treatment than a north-facing window in a cold climate, and a bedroom requires more light-blocking capability than a living room.

Blinds use horizontal or vertical slats that tilt to control light and can be raised or drawn to fully expose the window. Horizontal blinds, typically aluminum, wood, or faux wood, are the most common and versatile treatment. The slat width ranges from 1/2-inch micro blinds to 2-1/2-inch plantation-style blinds. Wider slats provide a cleaner view between slats when open. Vertical blinds use fabric or PVC vanes that traverse on a track, making them the standard for sliding glass doors and wide window expanses. Shades are fabric panels that raise and lower but do not tilt. Roman shades fold into horizontal pleats when raised, balloon shades gather into billowing folds, and roller shades wrap around a tube mechanism. Cellular or honeycomb shades have an internal air pocket structure that provides the highest insulation value of any fabric treatment.

Shutters are rigid panels mounted within the window frame, available in wood, composite, or vinyl. Plantation shutters with wide louvers are a premium permanent treatment that adds architectural character and resale value. Interior shutters block more light than any fabric treatment and provide excellent insulation when closed. Curtains are lightweight, unlined fabric panels hung from a rod, offering filtered light and a casual aesthetic. Drapes are heavier, lined fabric panels that extend to the floor, often with pleated headings. They provide the most formal appearance and the best insulation when constructed with thermal lining and hung from floor to ceiling, creating a dead air space between the fabric and the window glass.
