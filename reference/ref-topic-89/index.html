Lead Paint Safety Requirements

EPA RRP Rule Overview

The Environmental Protection Agency's Renovation, Repair, and Painting Rule, enacted in 2010, requires that contractors performing renovation, repair, and painting projects that disturb lead-based paint in homes, child care facilities, and schools built before 1978 be certified and follow specific work practices to prevent lead contamination. Lead is a potent neurotoxin particularly harmful to children under age six, causing developmental delays, learning disabilities, and behavioral problems. The RRP rule aims to eliminate childhood lead poisoning from renovation activities.

Contractors must be EPA-certified through an accredited training provider and must assign a certified renovator to each covered project. The certified renovator is responsible for ensuring that lead-safe work practices are followed. Before beginning work, contractors must provide homeowners and occupants with the EPA's Renovate Right pamphlet and obtain written acknowledgment of receipt. The work area must be contained using 6-mil polyethylene sheeting extending at least 6 feet in all directions from the area where paint will be disturbed. Interior containment requires plastic sheeting on floors, sealing of doors and HVAC vents, and posting of warning signs.

Prohibited work practices under the RRP rule include open-flame burning or torching of lead-based paint, the use of heat guns operating above 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit, and power sanding or grinding without HEPA vacuum attachments. Acceptable methods include wet sanding, chemical stripping, and HEPA-equipped power tools. After work is completed, the area must be thoroughly cleaned using HEPA vacuums and wet wiping. The certified renovator must perform a visual cleaning verification or, in some cases, dust wipe sampling to confirm that lead dust levels are below EPA clearance standards. Waste must be contained in heavy-duty plastic bags and disposed of according to local regulations. Records of compliance must be maintained for three years. Violations can result in fines up to $37,500 per violation per day.
