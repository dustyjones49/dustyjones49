Mulch Types Comparison

Selecting Landscape Mulch

Mulch is a critical component of healthy landscape beds, providing weed suppression, moisture retention, soil temperature moderation, and organic matter as it decomposes. The choice of mulch material affects not only aesthetics but also soil chemistry, plant health, and long-term maintenance costs. Organic mulches break down over time and improve soil structure; inorganic mulches are permanent but do not contribute to soil health.

Hardwood bark mulch is the most widely used organic mulch in residential landscaping. Made from shredded oak, maple, and other hardwoods, it has a rich brown color that weathers to gray over the course of a season. Hardwood mulch decomposes in 1 to 2 years depending on particle size and climate, releasing nutrients into the soil as it breaks down. It provides excellent weed suppression at a 2- to 3-inch depth and retains moisture effectively. Dyed hardwood mulches in black, red, or brown retain their color for a full season but may contain chemical dyes that some gardeners prefer to avoid. Pine bark mulch, available in nuggets, mini-nuggets, and shredded forms, decomposes more slowly than hardwood, lasting 2 to 3 years. Its natural reddish-brown color and pleasant pine aroma make it popular for foundation plantings and around acid-loving plants.

Cypress mulch is valued for its attractive blonde color and slow decomposition rate. However, environmental concerns about cypress wetland harvesting have reduced its popularity, and many landscapers now recommend pine bark or hardwood as sustainable alternatives. Rubber mulch, made from recycled tires, is a permanent inorganic mulch that does not decompose. It provides excellent weed suppression and impact absorption under playground equipment but can heat up significantly in direct sun and may leach zinc and other compounds into the soil over time. Stone mulch, including river rock, lava rock, and crushed gravel, is the most durable option with a lifespan measured in decades. Stone mulch is ideal for dry-climate xeriscapes, drainage areas, and around fire-prone homes where organic mulch poses a fire risk. Stone retains heat and reflects sunlight, which can stress some plants.
