How to Build a Backyard Fire Pit

Overview

A permanent fire pit built from retaining wall blocks creates a beautiful and durable backyard gathering spot. The project takes 4—6 hours and costs $300—600 for materials including the block, gravel, steel fire ring, and seating area gravel. Check local fire codes and HOA rules before starting — many municipalities require fire pits to be at least 15—25 feet from structures and property lines, and some ban wood-burning pits entirely in favor of gas-only.

Tools & Materials

  • Retaining wall blocks (36—48 wedge-shaped blocks for a 36"—44" inner diameter fire pit, 3—4 courses high)
  • Steel fire ring insert (optional but strongly recommended — protects blocks from heat cracking)
  • Paver base gravel (3/4" crushed stone, 2—3 cubic feet for base layer)
  • Landscape block adhesive (for top course), level, and rubber mallet
  • Shovel, tamper (hand tamper or plate compactor rental), measuring tape, marking paint or rope, wheelbarrow

Step-by-Step

  1. Choose and mark the location: Select a flat area at least 25 feet from buildings, trees, fences, and overhead branches. Call 811 before digging to have underground utilities marked. Mark a circle using a stake in the center and a string — typically 44"—48" outer diameter for a 36" inner fire area. For a square pit, mark a 48" × 48" square.
  2. Excavate and prepare the base: Remove sod and dig 4—6" down across the entire pit footprint plus 6" beyond the outer block face. Level the excavated area and compact the soil with a hand tamper. Add 2—3" of paver base gravel and tamp thoroughly until fully compacted. Check for level in multiple directions — every course of blocks you add will amplify any out-of-level in the base.
  3. Lay the first course: Place the first course of blocks in a circle on the compacted gravel base. This is the most important course — check every block for level and adjust with a rubber mallet and additional gravel as needed. Use a level across adjacent blocks and across the entire circle. For wedge-shaped blocks, the narrow edge faces inward to create the circular shape.
  4. Stack subsequent courses: Offset the second course so each block straddles the seam between two blocks below (running bond pattern). This creates interlocking strength. Check level on every block. Apply a bead of landscape block adhesive between each block in the second course to lock them in place — this prevents shifting from freeze-thaw cycles.
  5. Apply top course adhesive: Before placing the final course, run a continuous bead of landscape block adhesive along the entire top of the second-to-last course. Set the final course blocks in place, pressing down firmly. The adhesive creates a permanent bond. Wipe away any excess that squeezes out.
  6. Install the steel fire ring (recommended): Place a 36" diameter steel fire ring insert inside the block circle. This protects the blocks from direct flame contact and extreme heat that can cause cracking. The ring also catches ash and coals for easier cleanup. Fire rings cost $60—120 and extend the fire pit's life by years.
  7. Add gravel to the pit floor: Spread 2" of pea gravel or lava rock inside the fire pit floor — never use river rock, which can trap water and explode when heated. The gravel promotes drainage and provides a clean base for fires. Do not fill gravel above the bottom of the steel ring if using one.

Tips & Warnings

  • Always use retaining wall blocks or fire-rated blocks — never use standard concrete blocks, which can explode when heated due to trapped moisture.
  • Leave small gaps (1/4"—1/2") between blocks on the bottom course for airflow (unless instructions specify no gaps). Fire needs oxygen to burn clean and hot.
  • Cover the fire pit with a fitted cover when not in use. A water-logged fire pit creates a mess, extinguishes poorly, and accelerates block deterioration from freeze-thaw cycles.
  • Never use lighter fluid or gasoline to start a fire pit fire — use fire starters, kindling, and seasoned firewood only.
  • Keep a fire extinguisher, garden hose, or bucket of sand nearby whenever the fire pit is in use. Embers are unpredictable on windy days.
