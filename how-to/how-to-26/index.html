How to Install an Outlet

Overview

Installing a new electrical outlet in an existing wall is a common DIY project that adds convenience where you need power. This guide covers adding a receptacle to an existing circuit by tapping off another outlet box. Always shut off power at the breaker and verify with a voltage tester. If you are not comfortable working with electrical wiring, hire a licensed electrician. Permits may be required in your area.

Tools & Materials

  • 15A or 20A tamper-resistant outlet (match the circuit ampacity)
  • Old-work (cut-in) electrical box
  • 12/2 or 14/2 NM-B Romex cable (12-gauge for 20A, 14-gauge for 15A)
  • Non-contact voltage tester & multimeter
  • Wire strippers & needle-nose pliers
  • Drywall jab saw
  • Flathead & Phillips screwdrivers
  • Electrical tape
  • Cable ripper (or utility knife)

Step-by-Step

  1. Turn off power at the breaker. Identify the correct breaker and switch it to OFF. Use a non-contact voltage tester on the existing outlet to confirm power is off. Test both outlets of a duplex receptacle—they may be on different circuits.
  2. Choose the location. The new outlet must be at the same height as existing outlets—typically 12–16 inches to the bottom of the box. Locate it between two studs using a stud finder. Mark the electrical box outline on the wall with a pencil.
  3. Cut the opening. Use a drywall jab saw to cut along the traced outline. Cut at a slight inward angle so the box ears have solid drywall to grip. Test-fit the old-work box—it should slide in snugly.
  4. Run cable to the new box. Turn off power to the entire circuit at the breaker. Remove the cover plate from the existing outlet. Fish a length of Romex cable from the new opening to the existing outlet box. You may need to drill through studs if the boxes are not in the same stud bay. For same-bay installations, simply push the cable through.
  5. Secure the cable. Strip about 6 inches of the outer sheathing from the cable inside the new box using a cable ripper. Insert the cable through a knockout on the box. Tighten the cable clamp. Leave at least 6 inches of wire extending from the box per code.
  6. Mount the box. Slide the old-work box into the wall opening. Turn the mounting screws clockwise—the plastic wings behind the drywall will flip out and clamp the box against the drywall. Tighten until snug but do not overtighten or the drywall will crush.
  7. Wire the outlet. Strip about 3/4 inch of insulation from each conductor. Form hooks on the wire ends with pliers. Connect the black (hot) wire to the brass screw, white (neutral) to the silver screw, and bare copper (ground) to the green screw. Hook wires clockwise so tightening the screw pulls the wire in rather than pushing it out.
  8. Wire at the source box. In the existing outlet box, pigtail the new cable into the existing wiring: splice black to black, white to white, and all grounds together with a wire nut. Add a short pigtail from each splice to the existing outlet. Gently fold wires into the box so nothing gets pinched.
  9. Test and finish. Turn the breaker back on. Use a multimeter or outlet tester to verify correct polarity and grounding at both outlets. Mount the outlet flush into the box, install the cover plate, and turn the power back off one last time to do a final torque check on all connections.

Tips

Tip 1: Never back-stab an outlet (push wires into the back holes). Use the screw terminals—they make a far more reliable connection that will not arc over time.
Tip 2: Wrap electrical tape around the outlet body covering the screw terminals. This prevents accidental contact when pushing the outlet back into the box.
Tip 3: If the circuit is 20A (12-gauge wire, 20A breaker), you must use a 20A-rated outlet. On a 15A circuit, a 15A outlet is acceptable and standard.
Tip 4: Pigtailing (using a wire nut to connect the incoming, outgoing, and a short wire to the outlet) is preferred over using the outlet as a pass-through. It means a failed outlet does not kill everything downstream.
Tip 5: Aluminum wiring (common in homes built 1965–1973) requires special AL/CU-rated outlets and anti-oxidant paste. If you have aluminum wiring, consult an electrician.
