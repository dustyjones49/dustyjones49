How to Fix a Running Toilet

Overview

A constantly running toilet wastes up to 200 gallons of water per day and is almost always an easy DIY fix. The three most common causes are: a leaking flapper, an improperly adjusted fill valve, or a cracked overflow tube. Most repairs cost under $15 and take less than 30 minutes using basic tools.

Tools & Materials

  • Toilet flapper (universal fits most, but 2" or 3" flappers are common)
  • Fill valve kit (if original is old/leaking)
  • Adjustable wrench, pliers, screwdriver (flathead and Phillips)
  • Sponge or small cup (for emptying tank), rags, rubber gloves
  • White vinegar (for cleaning mineral deposits)

Step-by-Step

  1. Diagnose the source: Remove the tank lid and observe while the toilet runs. If water is siphoning into the overflow tube, the fill valve float is set too high or the fill valve is stuck open. If water trickles into the bowl through the flapper, the flapper isn't sealing. Put a few drops of food coloring in the tank — if color appears in the bowl within 15 minutes without flushing, the flapper is leaking.
  2. Adjust the fill valve: If water is flowing into the overflow tube, the water level is too high. Adjust the float lower so water stops 1/2" to 1" below the top of the overflow tube. On older ball-float valves, bend the float arm down. On newer Fluidmaster-style valves, pinch the adjustment clip and slide the float down the rod.
  3. Replace the flapper: Shut off the water at the wall valve and flush to empty the tank. Unhook the old flapper's ears from the flush valve ears and detach the chain from the flush lever. Install the new flapper by hooking ears onto the flush valve pegs and connecting the chain. Leave about 1/2" of slack in the chain — too tight prevents sealing, too loose won't lift the flapper fully.
  4. Clean or replace the flush valve seat: With the water off, run your finger around the flush valve seat at the bottom of the tank. If it's rough or has mineral buildup, scrub with fine steel wool or white vinegar. If the seat is cracked or pitted, you'll need to replace the entire flush valve — a more involved repair (1—2 hours).
  5. Replace the fill valve (if needed): If the fill valve continues to leak after adjustment, replace it. Shut off water, disconnect the supply line, remove the lock nut under the tank, and lift the old valve out. Install the new valve with the rubber cone washer inside the tank, tighten the lock nut, reconnect the supply line, and attach the refill tube to the overflow tube with the provided clip.
  6. Test and fine-tune: Turn water back on and let the tank fill. Adjust the float level so water stops about 1/2" below the overflow tube. Flush multiple times, adjusting the chain length and float height until the toilet flushes completely and the flapper seals reliably.

Tips & Warnings

  • A flapper that looks fine can still leak — the rubber hardens over time and won't conform to the seat. If the flapper is more than 3—5 years old, just replace it.
  • Never use chlorine bleach tablets in the tank — they destroy rubber flappers, gaskets, and seals within months.
  • If you hear the toilet randomly refill for a few seconds throughout the day (the "phantom flush"), the flapper is leaking and the fill valve is topping up the tank.
  • A hissing sound without visible water movement usually means the fill valve diaphragm is worn — the valve partially leaks and will eventually fail completely.
