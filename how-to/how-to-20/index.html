How to Cut Crown Molding

Overview

Cutting crown molding is intimidating because it sits at an angle against both the wall and ceiling. Two methods exist: cutting upside-down and backwards in the miter saw (the jig-free method), or using crown stops. Both work. You will waste molding learning the angles—buy an extra 10% and practice on offcuts first. Coping inside corners is mandatory for professional results.

Tools & Materials

  • Compound miter saw (10-inch or 12-inch)
  • Coping saw with fine-tooth blade (18–24 TPI)
  • Crown molding (pre-primed MDF or solid wood)
  • Measuring tape, pencil, speed square
  • 18-gauge brad nailer with 2-inch brads
  • Stud finder
  • Crown molding corner blocks (optional, for beginners)
  • Wood filler, caulk, and sandpaper

Step-by-Step

  1. Determine spring angle. Most crown molding has a 38-degree or 45-degree spring angle (the angle it sits off the wall). Standard 3⅝-inch crown is typically 45/45 (both sides 45 degrees). Check the profile on a framing square to confirm.
  2. Method 1: Cut upside-down and backwards. Place the crown molding upside-down on the miter saw table. The bottom edge (the wall side) rests on the saw fence, and the top edge (the ceiling side) rests on the saw table. The molding sits at its natural spring angle without special jigs. Set the miter angle to the corner angle divided by 2 (typically 45 degrees for a 90-degree corner).
  3. Method 2: Use a crown stop jig. Set your miter saw to the correct compound angle: for 45-degree spring angle crown in a 90-degree corner, set miter to 35.3 degrees and bevel to 30 degrees. For 38-degree spring angle, set miter to 31.6 and bevel to 33.9. Use a crown stop to hold the molding at the correct bed angle.
  4. Cut outside corners. For an outside corner, the left piece gets a right-hand miter, and the right piece gets a left-hand miter. Cut both pieces about 1/16 long—you can shave them shorter but cannot add length. Dry-fit and adjust by tenths of a degree until the miter is tight face-to-face.
  5. Cope inside corners. Butt the first piece square into the corner against both walls. For the mating piece, cut a 45-degree miter with the molding oriented as if making an inside miter cut. Use a coping saw to cut along the profile line where the face of the miter meets the contoured face. Test-fit the coped piece against the butted piece.
  6. Splice long runs. When a wall is longer than a single piece, join two pieces with a 30-degree scarf joint. Both ends are mitered at 30 degrees in opposite directions. The longer slope makes the joint less visible and allows for expansion.
  7. Nail to studs. Locate and mark studs. Nail through the crown into the wall studs at the bottom and into the ceiling joists or blocking at the top. Two nails per stud location. Use 2-inch 18-gauge brads for 3⅝-inch crown.
  8. Fill, sand, and caulk. Fill all nail holes. For outside corners, apply a tiny amount of wood filler to the miter and sand smooth. Caulk all seams—crown-to-wall, crown-to-ceiling, and all corner joints. A tight paint line over caulk makes everything look seamless.

Tips

Tip 1: Label each piece on the back with the wall number and direction (e.g., Wall 2, arrow left) immediately after cutting to avoid mix-ups.
Tip 2: Inside corners are rarely exactly 90 degrees—use an angle finder and adjust the miter by half the measured angle.
Tip 3: If you struggle with coping, use corner blocks at inside corners. They eliminate the need for perfect copes and add architectural detail.
Tip 4: Install crown with a helper. One person holds the 16-foot piece while the other nails. Crown is unwieldy and snaps easily if handled alone.
Tip 5: Cut the longest walls first. If you miscut a piece, it can often become a smaller piece for a shorter wall rather than becoming scrap.
