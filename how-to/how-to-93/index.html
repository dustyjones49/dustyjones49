How to Start a Lawn Mower After Winter

Overview

A mower that sat all winter with gas in the tank is the #1 reason for spring starting problems. Stale gas gums up the carburetor in as little as 30 days. The good news: most no-start issues are fixable in under an hour with basic troubleshooting.

Tools & Materials

  • Fresh gasoline (less than 30 days old)
  • Carburetor cleaner spray
  • Spark plug socket and ratchet
  • New spark plug (have one on hand)
  • Fuel siphon or turkey baster
  • Gas can for old fuel disposal
  • Screwdriver set
  • Work gloves and safety glasses
  • Starting fluid (ether-based, optional)

Step-by-Step

  1. Drain the old gas. Stale fuel is the most common problem. Siphon all gas from the tank into an approved container. Look at the fuel—if it's dark, cloudy, or smells varnish-like, it's bad. Dispose of it properly.
  2. Add fresh fuel. Fill the tank with fresh gasoline. If available, use ethanol-free gas or gas with fuel stabilizer. Ethanol-blended gas absorbs water over winter and causes hard starting.
  3. Check/replace the air filter. A clogged air filter chokes the engine of air. Remove the cover and inspect. If it's packed with dirt or oil-soaked, replace it. A foam filter can be washed with soapy water, dried, and re-oiled.
  4. Inspect the spark plug. Remove the spark plug with a spark plug socket. If the tip is black (carbon-fouled), wet with gas (flooded), or has worn electrodes, replace it. Gap a new plug to spec (typically 0.030") before installing.
  5. Check for spark. Reconnect the plug wire with the plug removed. Hold the metal threaded part of the plug against the engine block (with insulated pliers). Pull the starter cord. You should see a blue-white spark at the electrode. No spark means an ignition problem.
  6. Clean the carburetor. If fresh gas and a new spark plug don't help, remove the air filter, spray carburetor cleaner into the air intake for 2 seconds, and try starting. If it fires briefly and dies, the carburetor jets are clogged with varnish. Remove and clean the carburetor or replace it.
  7. Prime the engine. Push the primer bulb (red or black rubber button) 3–5 times. You should feel resistance and see fuel moving. If the bulb stays collapsed or feels empty, the primer line may be cracked.
  8. Try starting fluid (if needed). Remove the air filter and spray a 1-second burst of starting fluid into the carburetor throat. Pull the starter. If it fires, the engine is good but fuel delivery is the problem—focus on the carburetor.
  9. Start with the correct technique. Set the throttle to full choke (cold start). Pull the cord with a firm, fast motion. Once it fires, move the choke to half, then to run as the engine warms up. Don't yank the cord to its limit—short, fast pulls are more effective.

Pro Tips

Tip 1: Prevent this problem next year. At the end of the mowing season, either add fuel stabilizer and run the engine for 10 minutes, or drain all fuel and run the engine until it stalls from fuel starvation.
Tip 2: If the pull cord is extremely hard to pull, the engine may be hydro-locked with oil that leaked past the piston rings during winter storage. Remove the spark plug and pull the cord several times to eject oil from the cylinder.
Tip 3: A completely clogged carburetor can be replaced for about $20 on most mowers—often cheaper and faster than cleaning the old one.
Tip 4: Use only ethanol-free gas in small engines if available in your area. Ethanol attracts water and deteriorates rubber fuel lines and carburetor gaskets.
