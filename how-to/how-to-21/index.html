How to Hang Drywall

Overview

Hanging drywall is a two-person job for sheets larger than 4x4 feet. A 12x12-foot room with 8-foot ceilings requires about 12 sheets and takes 4–6 hours to hang with two people. Use 1/2-inch drywall for walls, 5/8-inch for ceilings and fire-rated applications. The goal is tight seams with no gaps over 1/8 inch and screwing off with the fastener slightly dimpled.

Tools & Materials

  • Drywall sheets (4x8 or 4x12 ft)
  • Drywall screws (1¼-inch coarse thread for wood studs)
  • Cordless drywall screw gun or drill with dimpler bit
  • Drywall T-square (48-inch)
  • Utility knife with plenty of extra blades
  • Drywall jab saw (for outlet cutouts)
  • Drywall lift (rental, for ceilings)
  • Stepping stool or drywall stilts
  • Drywall rasp for edge smoothing

Step-by-Step

  1. Inspect framing before starting. Check that all studs are straight and properly spaced (16 inches on center). Shim bowed studs or sister with straight lumber. Check that electrical boxes extend 1/2 inch proud of the studs to match drywall thickness.
  2. Measure and score drywall. Mark the cut line with a drywall T-square. Run a utility knife firmly along the line, cutting through the paper face. Bend the sheet back along the score line—the gypsum core snaps cleanly. Cut the back paper with the knife. Smooth rough edges with a rasp.
  3. Hang the ceiling first. Ceiling sheets go up before wall sheets so the wall sheets support the ceiling edges. Use a drywall lift or two helpers. Stagger ceiling joints so four corners never meet at one point. Screw into ceiling joists at 12-inch intervals along the edges and 16-inch intervals in the field.
  4. Hang wall sheets horizontally. For standard 8-foot walls, hang 4x8 sheets horizontally—this puts the beveled edges at a comfortable taping height and minimizes butt joints. Leave a 1/2-inch gap at the floor (drywall should not touch concrete, which wicks moisture).
  5. Stagger all joints. Offset vertical joints between rows by at least 16 inches. Never let a seam align with a door or window opening corner—this guarantees a crack. Cut sheets to shift the seam at least 8 inches away from opening edges.
  6. Cut out electrical boxes. Measure from the edge of the last hung sheet to each side of the box. Transfer measurements to the drywall sheet. Cut the opening with a jab saw or use a rotary cutout tool with a guide bit that tracks the inside of the box.
  7. Screw off the drywall. Use a screw gun or drill with a dimpler bit. Screws should be slightly recessed (dimpled) below the surface without breaking the paper. Space screws 12 inches apart on edges, 16 inches in the field. Drive any proud screws deeper—they will show through mud.
  8. Check for lippage. Run your hand across every seam. If one sheet is noticeably higher than the adjoining one, you have lippage. Fiberglass mesh tape can help bridge minor lippage, but severe lippage requires removing screws and shimming the low side.

Tips

Tip 1: Use a drywall screw gun with a depth-sensitive nosepiece set correctly once and every screw will be perfectly dimpled without breaking the paper.
Tip 2: A drywall lift rental ($40/day) is worth every penny for ceilings—it eliminates the exhausting and joint-damaging struggle of holding sheets overhead.
Tip 3: Hang sheets so beveled edges meet beveled edges along the long seams. The recess created by two bevels holds the tape and mud below the surface.
Tip 4: Snap a chalk line at stud centers to keep screws straight and avoid missing studs—a screw into air does nothing and leaves a hole to fill later.
Tip 5: Use mold-resistant purple or green drywall (instead of regular white) in bathrooms, laundry rooms, and basements to prevent moisture damage.
