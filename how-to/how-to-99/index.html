How to Aerate a Lawn

Overview

Core aeration pulls small plugs of soil from the lawn, relieving compaction and letting air, water, and nutrients reach grass roots. It's essential for lawns with heavy clay soil, high foot traffic, or thatch buildup. Aerate once per year—in fall for cool-season grasses, in late spring for warm-season grasses.

Tools & Materials

  • Core aerator (rent a walk-behind machine; manual aerators are exhausting on anything larger than a postage stamp)
  • Marking flags (to mark sprinkler heads, invisible fence wires, and shallow utilities)
  • Garden hose and sprinkler
  • Rake
  • Work gloves
  • Safety glasses
  • Lawn mower

Step-by-Step

  1. Time it right. Aerate when your grass type is actively growing so it recovers quickly. Cool-season grasses (fescue, bluegrass, rye): early fall. Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, zoysia, St. Augustine): late spring.
  2. Water the lawn 1–2 days beforehand. The soil should be moist but not saturated. Water deeply (about 1 inch) the day before. Tines penetrate moist soil easily and pull clean plugs. If the soil is dry and hard, tines barely scratch the surface.
  3. Mark all obstacles. Walk the lawn and place flags at every sprinkler head, valve box, shallow utility line, invisible pet fence wire, and drain grate. Aerator tines will destroy anything they hit.
  4. Mow the lawn short. Mow the grass to about 2 inches the day before aerating. Shorter grass makes it easier to see the ground and reduces resistance on the machine.
  5. Rent and familiarize yourself with the machine. Core aerators are heavy (150–250 lbs) and self-propelled. Read the operating instructions. The machine has a tine engagement lever and a transport position. Wear steel-toe boots if possible.
  6. Make two passes in perpendicular directions. Start along the longest edge and make straight passes, overlapping slightly. After covering the entire lawn, make a second pass at a 90-degree angle to the first. This creates 20–40 holes per square foot—the recommended density.
  7. Avoid turning with tines engaged. When you reach the end of a pass, disengage the tines before turning the machine. Turning with tines in the ground tears up the turf.
  8. Troubleshoot impossible spots. For steep slopes, use the machine to aerate as far as safely possible, then finish edges and slopes with a manual step-on aerator or pitchfork.
  9. Leave the plugs on the lawn. Don't rake up the soil cores. They'll break down naturally within 1–2 weeks as they're hit by rain, irrigation, and the next mowing. The soil in the plugs contains beneficial microbes that help decompose thatch.
  10. Water after aerating. Give the lawn a light watering immediately after to settle displaced soil and reduce stress on the grass.

Pro Tips

Tip 1: Don't aerate a dormant lawn. Poking holes in stressed, dormant grass invites weeds and won't deliver the expected benefits. Wait until the grass greens up.
Tip 2: After aerating is the perfect time to overseed, fertilize, and add soil amendments (lime, gypsum, compost). The open holes give seeds direct soil contact and let nutrients bypass the thatch layer.
Tip 3: If you only want to aerate once every two years, split the rental cost with a neighbor and do both lawns the same day.
Tip 4: Don't bother with liquid "aeration" products or spiked sandals. They don't remove cores and can actually increase compaction. Mechanical core removal is the only proven method.
