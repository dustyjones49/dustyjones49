How to Unclog a Drain

Overview

A slow or clogged sink or tub drain is almost always a DIY fix. Start with the least invasive method (boiling water for grease clogs, plunger for soap/hair clogs) and escalate if needed. Chemical drain cleaners should be a last resort — they rarely work on complete blockages, damage pipes, and create a hazardous work environment if you later have to disassemble pipes.

Tools & Materials

  • Cup plunger (flat bottom for sinks; flanged plunger for toilets)
  • Drain snake / hand auger (25' is sufficient for most residential drains)
  • Channel-lock pliers, bucket, old rags
  • Baking soda, white vinegar, boiling water
  • Rubber gloves, zip-it or plastic drain cleaning strip (for hair in bath drains)

Step-by-Step

  1. Remove visible debris: Remove the sink stopper or pop-up assembly. Most bathroom sinks have a pop-up plug that unscrews from the drain neck or has a release clip under the sink on the lift rod. Use needle-nose pliers to pull out visible hair and debris. For tubs, remove the trip lever or overflow plate and pull out the linkage with the attached hair and soap accumulation.
  2. Boiling water and baking soda/vinegar: Pour a pot of boiling water down the drain. Then add 1/2 cup baking soda followed by 1/2 cup white vinegar. Cover the drain opening immediately and wait 15 minutes (the fizzing pushes through the clog). Flush with another pot of boiling water. This works well for soap scum and light grease clogs.
  3. Plunge correctly: Fill the sink or tub with 1—2 inches of water (water transmits force better than air). Cover the overflow hole with a wet rag to maintain suction. Center the plunger over the drain and pump vigorously for 15—20 seconds. Pull the plunger off — water should rush down if the clog has broken free. Repeat 2—3 times if needed.
  4. Snake the drain: Insert the snake cable into the drain and turn the handle clockwise while gently pushing forward. When you feel resistance, you're at the clog. Keep rotating the cable to chew through the clog while applying light pressure. When the cable moves freely, pull it back while still rotating (this brings debris out). Run hot water for 3—5 minutes to flush remaining debris.
  5. Remove and clean the P-trap: If snaking doesn't clear the blockage, place a bucket under the P-trap and unscrew the slip nuts with channel-lock pliers. Remove the trap and clean out the gunk inside with a bottle brush and hot water. The trap is a primary collection point for debris. Inspect the wall drain stub while the trap is off — snake from this access point if needed.
  6. For grease clogs (kitchen): Kitchen drains clog primarily from grease that solidifies in the pipes. Hot water and degreasing dish soap (not just water) should be your first attempt. For persistent grease clogs, you may need to snake the drain branch or roof vent. Avoid pouring cooking oil down the drain — wipe pans with a paper towel and discard grease in the trash.

Tips & Warnings

  • Never pour boiling water directly into a porcelain sink — the thermal shock can crack the porcelain. Run hot tap water first to warm the sink, or pour boiling water through a metal strainer.
  • If snaking from a sink, remove the P-trap and go through the wall stub. Snakes can damage the trap's thin metal or plastic interior.
  • If multiple fixtures are clogged simultaneously, the blockage is likely a main line issue that requires a professional with a powered auger.
  • After any drain cleaning, run hot water for 5—10 minutes to flush loosened debris completely down the line.
  • Install mesh strainers in all sink and tub drains to catch hair and debris before they enter the pipes — this prevents 90% of clogs.
