How to Tape Drywall Joints

Overview

Taping drywall joints is the first of three stages in finishing drywall (tape, block coat, skim coat). Proper taping prevents cracks along seams and creates a monolithic wall surface. For DIYers, paper tape is more forgiving than mesh because it has a factory crease that centers on flats and can be folded for inside corners. Budget 2–3 hours for a 12x12 room.

Tools & Materials

  • Paper drywall tape (pre-creased)
  • All-purpose joint compound (mud—premixed in a bucket)
  • 6-inch drywall knife
  • 4-inch drywall knife (for corners)
  • Mud pan or hawk
  • Utility knife
  • Inside corner trowel or corner knife
  • Mixing paddle & drill (to re-whip bucket mud)

Step-by-Step

  1. Check all screws are dimpled. Run a 6-inch knife over every screw and seam. If the knife catches on a proud screw, drive it deeper with the screw gun. If the paper is torn around a screw, remove it and drive a new screw nearby.
  2. Pre-fill large gaps. Gaps over 1/4 inch between sheets need pre-filling before tape. Fill with setting-type compound (powder you mix with water, like Sheetrock 45) because it dries harder and shrinks less than premixed mud. Let the pre-fill dry before taping.
  3. Mix the mud. Premixed joint compound settles in the bucket. Re-whip it with a drill and mixing paddle until smooth and creamy, adding a tiny splash of water if needed. Properly mixed mud spreads easily without dragging. Fill your mud pan about half full.
  4. Apply mud for flat seams. Using a 6-inch knife, spread a smooth, even band of mud about 4–6 inches wide along the seam, centered on the joint. The mud bed should be 1/16 to 1/8 inch thick across the entire seam. Do not leave bare spots where the tape will not adhere.
  5. Embed the tape. Unroll paper tape along the seam, centering the factory crease on the joint. Press the tape into the mud bed with your fingers, then firmly wipe down the tape with the 6-inch knife held at about a 30-degree angle. Work from the center outward to squeeze excess mud and air bubbles from under the tape. The tape should be flat with no blisters.
  6. Wipe off excess mud. Apply a final light skim of mud over the tape, just enough to cover it, and wipe it nearly clean with the knife. The goal at this stage is to embed the tape, not fill the bevel. A thin coat dries faster and reduces edge ridges.
  7. Tape inside corners. Fold paper tape along its center crease. Apply mud to both sides of the corner with a 4-inch knife. Press the folded tape into the corner, then wipe each side clean. An inside corner trowel makes this much easier and more consistent.
  8. Tape butt joints. Butt joints (the 4-foot ends of sheets) have no bevel and will create a hump. Apply mud, embed the tape, wipe, and feather the edges as much as possible. Butt joints will need wider feathering in later coats to hide the bump.
  9. Let tape dry completely. Allow the tape coat to dry for at least 12–24 hours before applying the block coat. Wet tape under a second coat of mud will bubble and delaminate. Use a fan to speed drying if needed.

Tips

Tip 1: If you see bubbles under the tape after it dries, slice them out with a utility knife and re-tape those spots—mudding over them only makes them bigger.
Tip 2: Paper tape is stronger than mesh and resists cracking better. Mesh tape requires setting-type compound for the first coat, which adds complexity for DIYers.
Tip 3: Keep your mud pan and knife clean. Dried crusts in the mud pan will gouge lines through your fresh mud bed every single time.
Tip 4: Do not use mesh tape on inside corners—it will crack. Paper tape folded into corners preserves the angle and improves fire ratings.
Tip 5: Add a few drops of dish soap to the mud for the taping coat. It acts as a surfactant, helping the mud flow better and reducing friction under the knife.
