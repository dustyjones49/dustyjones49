How to Sand Drywall

Overview

Sanding drywall is the dusty, tedious payoff for good mud work. The less you have to sand, the better your mudding was. Use a pole sander for broad flats and a sanding sponge for inside corners and detail work. Drywall dust is extremely fine, gets into everything, and should be controlled. Budget 1–2 hours for sanding a 12x12 room. Always wear a respirator.

Tools & Materials

  • Pole sander with 120-grit or 150-grit mesh screens
  • Sanding sponge (medium/fine grit)
  • Dust mask (N95 minimum) or full respirator
  • Safety glasses or goggles
  • Bright work light (500+ lumens)
  • Shop vac with fine-dust filter and bags
  • Plastic sheeting and painter's tape (to seal doorways)
  • Dust containment poles or zip wall system

Step-by-Step

  1. Seal off the room. Tape plastic sheeting over doorways, HVAC vents, and any openings. Drywall dust is so fine it travels through air currents for days. Set up a box fan in a window blowing outward to create negative pressure inside the room.
  2. Put on PPE. Wear an N95 mask at minimum; a half-face respirator with P100 filters is far better. Wear safety glasses sealed against your face. Drywall dust is alkaline and will irritate your eyes and lungs within minutes.
  3. Inspect walls with a work light. Hold a bright flashlight or work light flat against the wall, raking the beam across the surface. This reveals every ridge, hump, and imperfection. Circle problem areas with a pencil so you do not miss them while sanding.
  4. Sand flat seams with a pole sander. Use 120-grit mesh sanding screens on a pole sander. Apply even pressure and use long, sweeping strokes along the seam. The mesh screen resists clogging unlike sandpaper. Feather the edges of the mud into the surrounding drywall.
  5. Sand butt joints. Butt joints require more attention—the hump created by the joint must be feathered out 20–24 inches wide. Use a pole sander with 120-grit, sanding in long strokes that arc from the center outward to feather the edges into the wall surface.
  6. Hand-sand inside corners. Use a medium-grit sanding sponge to smooth inside corners. Fold the sponge to fit into the corner. Sand in one direction, not back and forth. Be careful not to round over the corner bead on outside corners.
  7. Screw heads and touch-ups. Rub a sanding sponge in a circular motion over each mudded screw head until flush. Check for ridges around the screw that the knife left behind. Sand these smooth.
  8. Vacuum all surfaces. Vacuum walls from top to bottom with a shop vac and wide brush attachment. Vacuum the floor. Dust remaining on the wall will mix with primer and create a gritty texture. After vacuuming, wipe critical areas with a barely damp microfiber cloth.
  9. Final light check. Rake the work light across walls again. Any remaining imperfections must be touched up with additional mud before priming—primer highlights flaws, it does not hide them.

Tips

Tip 1: Sanding mesh screens last 5 times longer than sandpaper because the open weave allows dust to pass through instead of clogging.
Tip 2: Use a dust-attachment pole sander that connects to your shop vac—it captures 90% of dust at the source before it becomes airborne.
Tip 3: Do not use a random orbital sander on drywall. The gypsum dust will destroy the motor bearings within minutes and the results will be wavy.
Tip 4: If the dust is overwhelming, try wet-sanding with a damp sponge for the final skim coat. It eliminates dust entirely but requires a gentle touch.
Tip 5: Change your shop vac filter after sanding a big room. The fine dust clogs the pleats permanently, and a clogged filter burns out the vac motor on the next use.
