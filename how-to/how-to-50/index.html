How to Build a Retaining Wall

Overview

A retaining wall holds back soil on a slope, prevents erosion, and creates usable flat space in a yard. For walls under 4 feet tall, interlocking concrete blocks (no mortar) are the most DIY-friendly option. The key to a long-lasting wall is the buried base course, proper drainage behind the wall, and backfill with gravel—not dirt. Budget 2—3 weekends for a 3-foot-tall, 20-foot-long wall. Walls over 4 feet may require engineering and a permit.

Tools & Materials

  • Interlocking retaining wall blocks (calculate surface area plus 5% waste)
  • Wall caps (if your block system has them)
  • Crushed gravel base (3/4-inch clear angular stone)
  • Drainage gravel (clean 3/4-inch stone for behind the wall, 12 inches wide)
  • Perforated drain pipe (4-inch with filter sock)
  • Landscape fabric (filter fabric)
  • Masonry chisel, 3-lb sledgehammer, dead-blow hammer
  • 4-foot level, string line, stakes, measuring tape
  • Plate compactor (rental), tamper (hand tamper for small work)
  • Circular saw with diamond blade (for cutting blocks)

Step-by-Step

  1. Call 811 and lay out the wall. Have underground utilities marked. Use stakes, string, and marking paint to lay out the wall line. For a curved wall, lay a garden hose along the path and mark with paint. The trench width should be twice the block depth.
  2. Excavate the trench for the base. Dig a trench 12—24 inches wide and deep enough to accommodate 6 inches of gravel base plus 1 inch of sand. The first course of blocks will be partially or fully buried—the taller the wall, the more buried courses you need. The absolute minimum is one full block plus 4—6 inches of base buried below grade.
  3. Compact and level the gravel base. Pour 6 inches of angular crushed gravel (not rounded pea gravel—it does not compact). Compact with a plate compactor in 2-inch lifts. Check level repeatedly. The entire wall depends on a level base course—a 1/4-inch error at the base becomes visible bowing by the top of the wall.
  4. Add the leveling sand. Spread a 1-inch layer of coarse sand on top of the compacted gravel base. Screed it flat. Do not use this sand as a fill layer for uneven base; the gravel underneath must already be dead level.
  5. Set the first course of blocks. Place each block directly on the sand bed. Use a dead-blow hammer and a 4-foot level to set each block. Check level front-to-back and side-to-side. Check level across multiple adjacent blocks. This is the most time-consuming step—every block above relies on the first course being absolutely perfect.
  6. Install drainage behind the wall. Run landscape fabric from the back of the trench base up the back side. Lay a 4-inch perforated drain pipe behind the first course, sloping toward one end where it daylights or drains to a lower area. Fill the space behind the blocks with clean drainage gravel to a depth of 12 inches, compacted in lifts.
  7. Stack subsequent courses. Offset each row by half a block so vertical joints do not align. For pinned systems, tap the blocks together until the pins click. For lipped blocks, pull each block forward until the lip sits against the course below. Check level every few blocks. Backfill with gravel after each course—never stack more than two courses before backfilling.
  8. Install wall caps. Once the wall reaches the desired height, install caps on the top course. Caps are adhered with construction adhesive (landscape block adhesive). Apply two beads of adhesive per block, set the cap, and let cure for 24 hours. Adhesive prevents caps from being knocked off by foot traffic or mowers.
  9. Backfill the top and grade. Fold the landscape fabric over the top of the gravel backfill. Backfill the final 6—12 inches with soil and topsoil. Grade the soil to slope away from the wall so surface water runs away rather than ponding behind the blocks. Seed or sod.

Tips

Tip 1: Every retaining wall needs a slight backward lean (batter) of about 1 inch for every 12 inches of height. This counteracts soil pressure. Most interlocking blocks have a built-in lip that sets the batter automatically.
Tip 2: The drain pipe behind the wall is NOT optional. Without drainage, hydrostatic pressure builds up behind the wall after rain and can push the entire wall over in a single storm.
Tip 3: Hold a string line along the front face to keep it perfectly straight. Your eye can only detect 1/8 inch of misalignment, and a string line catches it immediately.
Tip 4: Walls over 4 feet tall require engineering and should be designed by a structural engineer. The force on the wall increases exponentially with height.
Tip 5: Cut blocks with a diamond blade on a circular saw—but make all cuts in the rear half of blocks whenever possible. Full block faces on the visible surface look professional; cut edges look amateur.
