How to Mud Drywall

Overview

Mudding (finishing) drywall is applying joint compound over tape and fasteners to create a smooth, seamless surface. It is done in three stages after taping: the block coat, the fill coat, and the skim coat. Each coat gets progressively wider and thinner. Good mud work means minimal sanding. Budget 3–4 days for all coats including drying time for a 12x12 room.

Tools & Materials

  • All-purpose joint compound (premixed, light-weight preferred)
  • 6-inch, 10-inch, and 12-inch drywall knives
  • Mud pan and mixing paddle with drill
  • Inside corner trowel or corner knife
  • Mud hawk (optional but professional)
  • Wet sponge & utility knife
  • Work light (to rake across walls, revealing imperfections)
  • Setting-type compound (for deep fills)

Step-by-Step

  1. Scrape the tape coat. Before applying the block coat, run a 6-inch knife over all taped seams to knock off any ridges, lumps, or dried mud boogers from the tape coat. Do not sand—just scrape. The tape coat should be flat but not feathered.
  2. Apply the block (fill) coat on flat seams. Using a 6-inch knife, fill the beveled recess with mud, covering the tape and bringing the mud level with the drywall surface. Wipe the knife tight at the edges so there is no ridge. The goal is to fill the bevel, not build beyond it.
  3. Apply the block coat on butt joints. Use a 10-inch or 12-inch knife for the wider fill coat on butt joints. Apply a band of mud 10–12 inches wide, feathering the edges down to nothing. The center over the joint will be about 1/16 inch thicker than the edges. Let the block coat dry completely (24 hours).
  4. Scrape and apply the fill coat. Scrape any ridges from the dried block coat. Using a 10-inch knife on flats and a 12-inch knife on butts, apply the second coat wider than the first—about 10 inches on flats and 14–16 inches on butts. Feather the edges aggressively. Apply a second coat to all screw heads.
  5. Apply the skim coat. Thin the final batch of mud with a few ounces of water to a creamy, almost pancake-batter consistency. Using a 12-inch knife, apply a tight skim coat to all joints, fanning out 12–16 inches on flats and 20–24 inches on butts. The edges should be feathered so thin the knife leaves almost no mud on the surface.
  6. Coat inside corners. After the tape dries, apply mud to one side of the corner at a time using a corner trowel. Let one side dry, then coat the other side. Trying to coat both sides of a corner simultaneously smears wet mud onto dry mud.
  7. Spot prime with a work light. After the skim coat dries, rake a bright work light across the wall at a low angle. Circle any imperfections—craters, ridges, edges that did not feather out. Touch up these spots with a thin coat of mud.
  8. Apply a tight fill to screw heads. Screw heads need three coats: tape coat, block coat, and skim coat. Apply each coat by filling the dimple and wiping the knife tight in two perpendicular directions. Properly filled screws are invisible after painting.

Tips

Tip 1: Lightweight joint compound is easiest for a DIYer—it sands with half the effort of all-purpose and shrinks less.
Tip 2: Add a splash of water to premixed mud for the skim coat. Thinner mud feathers out to nothing much more easily than thick mud.
Tip 3: Do not try to get perfect results in one thick coat. Three thin coats drying and shrinking independently produce a far better result than one thick coat.
Tip 4: A mud hawk (a flat plate you hold) holds more mud and is faster than a mud pan for large areas. It takes practice but is worth learning.
Tip 5: The work-light rake test will show you everything that needs attention before priming. Rely on a bright LED work light, not the ambient room light.
