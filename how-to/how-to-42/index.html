How to Fix a Leaking Pipe Joint

Overview

A leaking pipe joint can cause significant water damage if not addressed quickly. The fix depends on the joint type: threaded joints usually leak because the sealant failed; soldered (sweated) joints leak because the solder cracked from vibration or freeze damage. Temporary emergency fixes (epoxy putty, repair clamps) can buy time but should be followed by a permanent repair. Always shut off water and drain the pipe before attempting any permanent fix.

Tools & Materials

  • Pipe wrenches (2) or adjustable wrenches
  • Teflon tape (yellow for gas, white for water)
  • Pipe joint compound (pipe dope)
  • Epoxy putty stick (steel-reinforced, for emergency patches)
  • Pipe repair clamp (stainless steel, matches pipe diameter)
  • Emery cloth or wire brush
  • Propane torch, flux, and lead-free solder (if re-soldering)
  • Bucket, towels, and rags

Step-by-Step

  1. Identify the leak location precisely. Dry the pipe completely with a rag. Wrap a dry paper towel around the joint and run the water—the exact wet spot shows precisely where the leak is. For threaded joints, leaks at the threads indicate failed sealant. For soldered joints, a pinhole leak indicates a crack in the solder ring.
  2. Shut off water and drain the pipe. Close the nearest valve or the main shut-off. Open the faucet downstream to drain all water from the pipe. A pipe with even a drop of water dribbling through cannot be soldered and will prevent epoxy from curing.
  3. For threaded joints: disassemble and re-seal. Hold the fitting with one wrench while loosening the pipe with the other (backup wrench technique prevents twisting the whole assembly). Once apart, clean both male and female threads with a wire brush or emery cloth until bright. Wrap male threads with Teflon tape (3–4 wraps clockwise), then brush on pipe dope. Reassemble and tighten 1–2 turns past hand-tight.
  4. For soldered joints: re-sweat the joint. Apply flux to the leaking area. Heat the joint with a propane torch—keep the flame moving around the fitting, not focused on one spot. When the flux bubbles and turns clear, touch the solder to the joint. If the metal is hot enough, solder will wick into the joint. Let cool for 10 minutes before pressurizing.
  5. Emergency fix: epoxy putty. For a temporary repair, shut off water and drain. Knead the epoxy stick until the color is uniform. Press firmly into the crack or pinhole, molding it around the pipe. Work the material slightly into the crack. Let cure for the full time stated (usually 1 hour for pressure). This is NOT a permanent fix.
  6. Emergency fix: pipe repair clamp. For a split or pinhole on straight pipe, place the rubber gasket over the leak, position the clamp's metal saddle over the gasket, and tighten the bolts evenly until snug. The clamp is a stronger temporary fix than epoxy and can last months if properly tightened.
  7. Test the repair. Slowly turn the water back on. Check the joint with a dry paper towel. If still leaking, tighten threaded joints another quarter-turn or re-solder soldered joints once more. If a threaded joint still leaks after two attempts, the threads may be crossed or damaged—the fitting must be replaced.

Tips

Tip 1: For threaded joints, always use both Teflon tape AND pipe dope together—tape fills the thread gap, dope lubricates and provides a secondary seal. Neither alone is as reliable.
Tip 2: When re-soldering a leaking joint, you must drain all water from the pipe. Use a shop vac on the open end to suck water out, or stuff white bread into the pipe temporarily to absorb water.
Tip 3: If a soldered joint leaks only when the pipe is hot (like a heating line), the leak is from thermal expansion and will need a professional to assess—the pipe length may need an expansion loop.
Tip 4: Epoxy putty does not work on wet, oily, or dirty pipes—that includes condensation from cold water pipes. Dry the pipe with a hair dryer just before applying putty for the best bond.
Tip 5: A repair clamp is rated for specific pipe diameters (nominal size). A 1/2-inch clamp will not fit 3/4-inch pipe even if tightened fully. Measure the pipe circumference and divide by 3.14 to find diameter.
