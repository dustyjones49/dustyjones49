How to Install Baseboard Trim

Overview

Baseboard trim hides the expansion gap at the floor-wall junction and gives a room a finished look. The difference between amateur and pro baseboard work is in the corners: pro results come from coping inside corners instead of mitering them. A 12x12-foot room takes about 3–4 hours for a DIYer. Paint or stain the baseboards before installing.

Tools & Materials

  • Baseboard trim (pre-primed MDF or solid wood)
  • Miter saw (compound sliding preferred)
  • Coping saw with fine-tooth blade
  • 18-gauge brad nailer with 2-inch brads
  • Stud finder
  • Hammer and nail set
  • Wood filler or spackle
  • Construction adhesive (optional)
  • Caulk (paintable latex)

Step-by-Step

  1. Measure each wall precisely. Measure from corner to corner along the floor. Mark the measurement on the baseboard. Label the back of each piece with the wall number and direction. Measure each wall individually—never assume walls are equal.
  2. Cut outside corners with a miter. For an outside corner, set the miter saw to 45 degrees. The piece on the left wall gets a right-facing 45, the piece on the right gets a left-facing 45. Dry-fit and adjust the saw angle in 0.5-degree increments until the joint is tight.
  3. Cope inside corners. For an inside corner, cut the first piece square and butt it into the corner. For the second piece, cut a 45-degree miter (as if making an inside miter), then use a coping saw to cut along the profile line, removing the miter face. The coped end fits perfectly over the adjoining piece's profile.
  4. Scribe for uneven floors. If the floor is uneven, set the baseboard in place and use a compass to scribe the floor contour onto the bottom edge. Plane or sand to the scribe line so the baseboard sits flush with no gaps.
  5. Locate studs and mark. Baseboard nailing goes into wall studs and the bottom plate. Studs are typically at 16-inch centers. Mark stud locations on the wall above where the baseboard will cover them.
  6. Nail the baseboard. Use an 18-gauge brad nailer with 2-inch brads. Shoot nails at a slight downward angle at stud locations—two nails per stud, one near the top and one near the bottom. On long runs, add a dab of construction adhesive to the back for extra hold.
  7. Join sections with scarf joints. For walls longer than a single piece of trim, join two pieces with a 30-degree scarf joint (miter both ends at 30 degrees in opposite directions). The joint is less visible than a butt joint and allows for wood movement.
  8. Set nails and fill holes. Use a nail set to sink any proud brad heads slightly below the surface. Fill every nail hole and any corner gaps with wood filler or spackle. Let dry, then sand smooth with 120-grit.
  9. Caulk the top edge. Run a thin bead of paintable latex caulk along the top of the baseboard where it meets the wall. Smooth with a damp finger. Do not caulk the bottom edge to the floor unless the floor is extremely uneven—the baseboard needs to move with seasonal changes.

Tips

Tip 1: Coping inside corners eliminates gaps from seasonal wood movement—miters always open up over time, coped joints do not.
Tip 2: Prime the back side of baseboards as well as the front, especially in bathrooms. Unprimed backs absorb moisture and warp over time.
Tip 3: A coping foot on a jigsaw ($20 accessory) is faster than a hand coping saw once you have practice.
Tip 4: Use a scrap piece of baseboard flipped upside down against the wall to scribe for uneven floors—no compass needed.
Tip 5: Buy 16-foot lengths even if you have a small car. Most stores will cut them to fit, and you get fewer joints than with 8-footers.
