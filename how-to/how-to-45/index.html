How to Adjust Water Pressure

Overview

Water pressure in a home should be between 40–60 PSI for comfortable use without damaging pipes and appliances. Pressure above 80 PSI strains pipes, causes water hammer, and shortens the life of washing machines, dishwashers, and water heaters. If you have low pressure, a partially closed valve or clogged aerator may be the cause. If pressure is high, a pressure-reducing valve (PRV) needs adjustment or replacement. Budget 30 minutes for diagnosis and adjustment.

Tools & Materials

  • Water pressure gauge (screw-on hose bib type, $10–$15)
  • Adjustable wrench
  • Flathead screwdriver
  • Teflon tape
  • Replacement faucet aerators (if low pressure at one fixture)
  • Pressure reducing valve (PRV) if replacement is needed
  • Bucket & towel

Step-by-Step

  1. Measure the water pressure. Screw the pressure gauge onto a hose bib or laundry sink faucet. Open the valve fully. Read the static pressure (no water running elsewhere). Take a second reading with a faucet running elsewhere in the house—this is the dynamic pressure and should not drop more than 10 PSI.
  2. Determine if pressure is too high or too low. Ideal: 40–60 PSI. Acceptable: up to 75 PSI. Too high: 80+ PSI (needs a PRV adjustment). Too low: below 30 PSI (check for closed valves, PRV failure, or municipal supply issues).
  3. If pressure is high: locate the PRV. The pressure-reducing valve is usually on the main water line where it enters the house, often near the main shut-off or in the basement. It is a bell-shaped brass valve with a bolt and locknut on top.
  4. Adjust the PRV. Loosen the locknut on top of the PRV with a wrench. Turn the adjustment bolt clockwise to increase pressure, counterclockwise to decrease it. Adjust in quarter-turn increments. Run a faucet to cycle the pressure, then re-check with the gauge. Repeat until pressure is in the 50–60 PSI range. Tighten the locknut when done.
  5. If pressure is low in one fixture only. Check the faucet aerator—unscrew it and clean mineral deposits with vinegar. Check the shut-off valve under the sink is fully open. If one hot water faucet has low pressure, the hot water heater outlet or the faucet cartridge may be clogged.
  6. If pressure is low throughout the house. Check that the main shut-off valve is fully open (gate valves sometimes fail partially closed). Check the PRV if you have one—it may have failed. Check for a whole-house filter that may be clogged. If none of these resolve it, the municipal supply pressure may be low—call the water utility to check pressure at the meter.
  7. If the PRV is leaking or cannot maintain pressure. A PRV that is 10+ years old, covered in mineral deposits, or leaking from the top stem is failing. Replace it ($50–$100) or have a plumber replace it. This requires shutting off water at the main.
  8. Final measurement. After adjustment, screw the gauge back on and verify the static pressure is within range. Record the reading and date on a tag attached to the PRV for future reference.

Tips

Tip 1: A pressure reading taken at night when no one in the neighborhood is using water may read 10–15 PSI higher than daytime dynamic pressure.
Tip 2: If pressure fluctuates wildly (60 PSI then 20 PSI), the PRV diaphragm is likely torn or the municipal supply cannot meet peak demand. A thermal expansion tank on the water heater can help stabilize.
Tip 3: A water pressure gauge is a tool every homeowner should own. Attach it once a year to a hose bib and check pressure—catching a failing PRV early prevents appliance damage.
Tip 4: Local plumbing codes require a PRV when incoming pressure exceeds 80 PSI. If your home lacks one and your pressure is high, install one—it is not optional.
Tip 5: After adjusting the PRV, flush all toilets and open all faucets for 30 seconds to cycle the new pressure through the system before trusting the gauge reading.
