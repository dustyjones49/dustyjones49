How to Run Electrical Wire Through Walls

Overview

Running electrical cable inside finished walls is the most challenging part of adding circuits or outlets. The key skill is fishing: using a flexible fiberglass rod or magnetic tool to pull cable through wall cavities, across ceilings, and past fire blocks. This guide focuses on fishing cable from an existing outlet down through the wall to a new outlet below it. Allow 1–2 hours for a simple same-bay run, longer if crossing stud bays or floors.

Tools & Materials

  • NM-B Romex cable (12/2 or 14/2 depending on the circuit)
  • Fish tape (25-foot steel or fiberglass)
  • Glow rods (fiberglass fish rods that screw together)
  • Flexible drill bit (54-inch installer bit with auger tip)
  • Right-angle drill (essential for drilling between studs in a wall)
  • Stud finder with AC wire detection
  • Drywall jab saw & utility knife
  • Magnetic wire puller or chain (for easier fishing)
  • Headlamp & inspection camera (optional but very helpful)

Step-by-Step

  1. Map the path. Determine the exact route: which stud bay, which floors or ceilings to cross, and where fire blocks or horizontal blocking might exist. Use a stud finder to locate studs. Check for plumbing, ductwork, or existing wires that might be in the way.
  2. Cut access holes. For long or complex runs, it is often easier to cut small strategic access holes in the drywall than to try blind fishing. A 4x4-inch hole patched later takes less time than hours of frustrated fishing.
  3. Drill through the top or bottom plate. If fishing from an attic or basement, use a flexible installer bit chucked into a right-angle drill. The bit is 54 inches long and can reach down inside the wall cavity to drill through the top or bottom plate. Drill a 5/8-inch hole centered on the plate for 12/2 or 14/2 cable.
  4. Feed the fish tape from the destination. Push the fish tape from the outlet opening or access hole toward the source. Tape the cable to the fish tape's hook with electrical tape, creating a smooth, tapered bullet shape so it will not snag on insulation or edges.
  5. Navigate fire blocks. If you hit a solid obstruction halfway up the wall, it is likely a fire block (horizontal 2x4 between studs). You must drill through it. Cut a small access hole at the fire block location, drill through it with the right-angle drill, then feed the cable through.
  6. Pull the cable. Once the fish tape reaches the destination, securely attach the cable and pull steadily from the other end. Have a helper feed the cable smoothly from the source side to prevent kinking. Pull 12–18 inches extra at both ends for working room.
  7. Secure the cable. NM cable must be stapled within 8 inches of a plastic box and at least every 4½ feet along framing. If fishing through existing walls without opening them, code allows you to forgo intermediate staples—the cable is considered fished and therefore does not need staples inside the wall cavity.
  8. Patch any access holes. Repair any drywall access holes with new drywall patches, tape, and mud. Sand and paint to match.
  9. Strip and terminate. At each end, strip the cable sheathing, leaving 6 inches of conductors in the box. Leave an inch of sheathing inside the box entrance. Wire the devices and test the circuit with a multimeter before calling the job complete.

Tips

Tip 1: Tie a small bolt to a string and drop it down the stud cavity from above. Use a magnet on the fish tape to grab the bolt through the drywall—this magnetic fishing technique works when you cannot see the end of the wire.
Tip 2: When drilling blind with a flexible bit, have a helper listen at the wall for the bit sound to guide your aim toward the target hole.
Tip 3: If the wall cavity is filled with insulation, use a flexible installer bit with a bullet tip to push through it, rather than a fish tape which will bind in fiberglass.
Tip 4: Keep cables at least 6 inches away from plumbing pipes in the wall. If a pipe ever leaks, the cable could carry current into the water.
Tip 5: An inspection camera attachment for your phone ($30) lets you look inside wall cavities to confirm the path is clear before drilling. This can save hours of trial and error.
