How to Snake a Drain

Overview

Snaking a drain uses a flexible steel cable (auger) to break up or retrieve clogs that a plunger cannot reach. For bathroom sinks and tubs, a 25-foot drum auger ($20–$40) handles most clogs beyond the P-trap. For main sewer lines, rent a heavy-duty 50–100 foot cable machine. Always remove the P-trap and snake directly down the drain line—never snake through the trap itself. Budget 30 minutes for a sink or tub, 1–2 hours for a main line.

Tools & Materials

  • Drum auger (25-foot, 1/4-inch cable for sinks and tubs)
  • Toilet auger (closet auger) for toilets only
  • Adjustable wrench & pipe wrench
  • Bucket & rags
  • Rubber gloves and safety glasses
  • Plumber's tape (Teflon)
  • Plumber's putty or pipe joint compound
  • Replacement P-trap washers (optional but wise)

Step-by-Step

  1. Remove the P-trap. Place a bucket under the trap. Loosen the slip nuts at both ends of the P-trap by hand or with pliers. Pull the trap off and empty its contents into the bucket. Clean the P-trap in a utility sink—often the clog is right here, not deeper in the line.
  2. Feed the auger cable into the drain pipe. With the P-trap removed, insert the auger cable into the wall drainpipe. Tighten the setscrew on the drum handle. Turn the handle clockwise while applying gentle forward pressure. The cable will advance on its own as it navigates bends.
  3. Work the cable through the clog. When you feel significant resistance that does not give way, you have reached the clog. Tighten the setscrew, then crank the handle aggressively while applying moderate pressure. The spinning tip will chew through organic clogs or twist around debris. You may feel the cable break through—sudden lack of resistance.
  4. Retrieve the cable. Once you are past the clog, run the cable 2–3 feet further to ensure the line is clear. Then reverse direction or loosen the setscrew and pull the cable back slowly. Keep a rag and bucket nearby—the cable will bring up material. Clean the cable with a rag as it comes out.
  5. For a toilet clog. Use a toilet auger (closet auger). Feed the bent end into the bowl drain, crank the handle clockwise while pushing forward. The protective rubber bend prevents the cable from scratching the porcelain. Push until resistance breaks, then retract.
  6. Flush the line. Before reassembling the P-trap, run hot water through the exposed drainpipe for 2–3 minutes to flush loosened debris. Use a garden hose carefully—it creates high pressure that could splash back. Alternatively, pour buckets of hot water.
  7. Reassemble and test. Reinstall the P-trap with new washers if the old ones are cracked. Tighten slip nuts by hand plus a quarter-turn with pliers. Run water for 2 minutes and check for leaks. Fill the sink and release to verify fast drainage under a full load.

Tips

Tip 1: A manual drum auger is far more effective than a chemical drain cleaner and far safer for your pipes. Chemical cleaners can erode metal traps and fail to break up solid clogs.
Tip 2: If the cable just spins without advancing, the tip is hanging up on a fitting or joint. Pull back a few inches and try again with a faster spinning motion.
Tip 3: For a completely blocked main drain (multiple fixtures backing up), do not rent a hand auger—the clog is too deep. Rent a motorized cable machine (50–100 feet heavy-duty) or call a plumber.
Tip 4: After snaking, run enzyme drain cleaner monthly for maintenance. It digests organic buildup without harsh chemicals and prevents clogs from re-forming.
Tip 5: If the auger retrieves roots, your sewer line has a crack or separated joint that tree roots have invaded. You need a plumber for a camera inspection and possible line replacement.
