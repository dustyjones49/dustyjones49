How to Overseed a Lawn

Overview

Overseeding fills thin and bare spots with new grass to crowd out weeds and restore density. The ideal time is early fall when soil is still warm from summer but air temperatures are cooling. Spring overseeding is possible but competes with germinating weed seeds.

Tools & Materials

  • Grass seed (choose a variety matched to your sun exposure and climate)
  • Broadcast spreader (push-type or hand-held)
  • Core aerator (rent one—dramatically improves seed-to-soil contact)
  • Power rake or dethatcher (if thatch exceeds 1/2")
  • Starter fertilizer (high phosphorus, e.g. 18-24-12)
  • Leaf rake (flexible tine, not a garden rake)
  • Peat moss, straw, or seed germination blanket (for moisture retention)
  • Lawn mower
  • Sprinkler with timer
  • Marking flags

Step-by-Step

  1. Choose the right seed. Match the seed to your lawn's conditions. Full sun: turf-type tall fescue or Kentucky bluegrass. Shade: fine fescue or shade-tolerant tall fescue mix. High traffic: perennial ryegrass. Warm climates: Bermuda or zoysia. Buy pure seed, not "quick fix" mixes with annual ryegrass that dies in one year.
  2. Prepare the lawn. Mow the existing grass short (1.5–2 inches) and bag the clippings. Short grass lets seeds reach the soil instead of getting caught in tall blades.
  3. Dethatch if needed. If thatch (the spongy layer of dead stems between grass and soil) is thicker than 1/2 inch, use a power rake or dethatcher. Rake up and remove the debris. Seed on top of thick thatch never reaches soil.
  4. Aerate the lawn. Run a core aerator across the lawn in at least two directions. Aeration punch-holes are the ideal seedbed—seeds fall into them, get soil contact, and are protected from wind and birds.
  5. Apply starter fertilizer. Spread a starter fertilizer with high phosphorus content to promote root development. Many states limit phosphorus due to runoff regulations—check local guidelines. Use a phosphorus-free starter if required.
  6. Spread the seed. Load the broadcast spreader with half the seed. Walk in one direction (e.g., north-south) spreading seed over the entire lawn. Load the remaining half and spread perpendicular (east-west). This crisscross pattern ensures even distribution. Hand-seed edges and tight spots.
  7. Rake lightly. Use the back side of a leaf rake to gently drag over the seeded area, working seeds into the aeration holes and making soil contact. Don't bury the seeds—they need light contact, not burial.
  8. Cover with a light mulch. Spread a thin layer (1/8" to 1/4") of peat moss, weed-free straw, or roll out a seed germination blanket over the seeded area. This retains moisture and reduces seed loss to birds and wind.
  9. Water—the critical step. Water lightly and frequently to keep the top 1/2" of soil constantly moist. This usually means 2–3 brief waterings (5–10 minutes each) per day for 2–3 weeks until germination. Never let the seedbed dry out—germinating seeds die within hours of drying.
  10. Gradually reduce watering. Once the new grass reaches 3 inches tall, reduce watering to once daily, then every other day, then transition to deep, infrequent watering (1 inch per week). Mow when the new grass reaches 3.5–4 inches, cutting no more than the top 1/3.

Pro Tips

Tip 1: Use a slice seeder (rental) instead of aerating + broadcasting if you want the highest germination rate with the least water. It cuts grooves and deposits seeds at the perfect depth in one pass.
Tip 2: Don't apply pre-emergent herbicide (crabgrass preventer) in the same season you overseed. Pre-emergent blocks all seeds from germinating, including grass seed.
Tip 3: Water on a timer with multiple short cycles. A programmable hose timer is the single best investment for overseeding success—missing one watering on a hot day can kill germinating seedlings.
Tip 4: Don't walk on the seeded lawn for 3–4 weeks. Foot traffic compacts the soil and crushes fragile seedlings before their roots anchor. If you must walk, use plywood boards to distribute weight.
Tip 5: Wait 6–8 weeks after overseeding before applying any weed killer. New grass is too tender to handle herbicides. Hand-pull weeds that emerge during this time.
