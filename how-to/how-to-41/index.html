How to Replace a Shut-Off Valve

Overview

Shut-off valves (angle stops) under sinks and toilets fail in two ways: they leak from the stem, or they seize completely and cannot be turned. Replacing one with a quarter-turn ball valve ($8–$15) is a 30–60 minute job. Two connection types exist: compression (clamps onto copper pipe with a ferrule) and push-to-connect (push-fit, for DIYers). Always shut off the main water supply before starting—this valve is the last defense before a flood.

Tools & Materials

  • Quarter-turn shut-off valve (1/2-inch compression or push-fit x 3/8-inch compression)
  • Adjustable wrench (2 recommended: one to hold, one to turn)
  • Ferrule puller or compression sleeve removal tool
  • Mini hacksaw or tubing cutter
  • Emery cloth or sanding mesh (120-grit)
  • Plumber's tape (Teflon)
  • Bucket & towels
  • Pipe joint compound (optional)

Step-by-Step

  1. Shut off the main water supply. Locate the main shut-off valve (typically in the basement, crawlspace, or near the water meter). Turn clockwise fully. Open a faucet at the lowest point in the house and a faucet at the highest point to drain the pipes. Confirm no water pressure at the valve you are replacing.
  2. Disconnect the supply line. Unscrew the supply line nut from the old valve. If the line is flexible braided stainless, it is reusable; if it is rigid chrome or old plastic, replace it. Place a bucket underneath.
  3. Remove the old valve. For a compression valve, loosen the compression nut with one wrench while holding the valve body with the other. Once loose, pull the valve straight off the pipe stub. A brass or copper ferrule (ring) will remain compressed onto the pipe.
  4. Remove the old ferrule (if compression). The old ferrule is crushed onto the pipe and will not slide off. Use a ferrule puller ($15) to extract it without damaging the pipe. If you do not have a puller, carefully cut the pipe behind the ferrule with a tubing cutter—but only if you have at least 1½ inches of pipe remaining to work with.
  5. Clean the pipe stub. Sand the end of the copper pipe with emery cloth until bright and smooth. Remove all burrs and old pipe dope. A rough or dirty pipe will not seal with a compression or push-fit fitting.
  6. Install the new valve. For compression: slide the compression nut onto the pipe, then the new brass ferrule. Insert the valve body onto the pipe, slide the nut and ferrule forward, and hand-tighten. Tighten with wrenches—the nut should turn 1/2 to 3/4 turn past hand-tight for a proper seal. For push-fit: simply push the valve onto the pipe until it clicks and bottoms out.
  7. Connect the supply line. Wrap the valve's outlet threads with 2–3 wraps of Teflon tape. Screw the supply line nut onto the valve and tighten with pliers. Connect the other end of the supply line to the faucet or toilet fill valve.
  8. Restore water and test. Make sure the new valve is in the OFF position. Turn the main water back on. Open the new valve slowly. Check all connections for leaks. If a compression fitting drips, tighten another eighth-turn. If it still drips, the ferrule did not seat properly—start over with a new ferrule.

Tips

Tip 1: Push-to-connect (SharkBite or similar) shut-off valves cost about $15 and remove the hardest part of the job—seating the ferrule. They are ideal for DIYers and have a lower failure rate for beginners.
Tip 2: Always replace a multi-turn shut-off valve with a quarter-turn ball valve. Multi-turn valves use a rubber washer that crumbles with age; ball valves rely on a stainless steel ball that never degrades.
Tip 3: Compression fittings seal metal-to-metal and do not need Teflon tape on the compression threads (only on the supply-line outlet threads). Tape on compression threads can prevent the nut from turning far enough.
Tip 4: If the copper pipe stub is too short (less than 1 inch protruding), you cannot get a compression or push-fit valve to seal. You will need to extend the stub with a coupling and a fresh piece of pipe, which requires soldering.
Tip 5: After installing, cycle the new valve on and off 3–4 times. New ball valves can be stiff initially; cycling them loosens the seal and spreads lubricant from the factory assembly.
