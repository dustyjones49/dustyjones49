Step-by-step guide to replacing a damaged deck board including removal without damaging adjacent boards, fastener cutting, matching new lumber, and staining to blend with existing deck.

How to Replace a Damaged Deck Board

Overview

A single cracked, split, or rotted deck board doesn't require replacing the entire deck. Individual board replacement takes about 45—60 minutes if the fasteners cooperate, or longer if they're rusted or stripped. The biggest challenge is removing the old board's fasteners without damaging adjacent boards. Composite decking uses hidden fasteners that require a different removal approach.

Tools & Materials

  • Replacement deck board (pressure-treated pine 5/4×6 or composite, matched to existing)
  • Crowbar or cat's paw nail puller, reciprocating saw with metal-cutting blade
  • Power drill with screwdriver bits, impact driver (helps with stubborn screws)
  • Deck screws (2-1/2" or 3" coated deck screws to match existing)
  • Saw (circular saw or jigsaw), measuring tape, carpenter's pencil

Step-by-Step

  1. Assess the board and fasteners: Determine how the board is attached — nails (common on older decks), face screws (most common), or hidden fasteners (composite/commercial). Also check if the board spans multiple joists or is joined with a butt joint somewhere mid-span. Take a photo of the board and surrounding boards' condition for reference.
  2. Remove fasteners: For face screws, back them out with a drill or impact driver. Rusted screws that strip require penetrating oil and patience, or must be cut from below. For nails, use a cat's paw or flat bar to pull them through. If nail/screw heads are stripped or impossible to back out, cut the board material away around the fastener, then cut the fastener shaft flush with a reciprocating saw — wear eye protection.
  3. Cut the board if spanning multiple joists: If the board is too long to pry up in one solid piece (fastened along multiple joists), cut it into 2—3 sections between joists with a reciprocating saw or circular saw set to board depth. Leave the cut sections resting on joists so they don't fall. Remove each section individually.
  4. Measure and cut the replacement: Measure the opening carefully — old boards may have been installed with a gap that has widened. Cut the new board to length. If the board has a butt joint, cut the new board precisely to fit, leaving a 1/8" gap at the joint and 1/4" at the ends. Pre-drill the board near the ends to prevent splitting.
  5. Install the new board: Set the new board in place. Use screws (never nails — they work loose). Drive 2-1/2" or 3" deck screws at every joist, two screws per joist per board. Keep screws 3/4" from board edges to prevent splitting. For the end screws, pre-drill through the board to avoid cracks. The new board's surface will sit slightly higher than surrounding boards until it weathers — this is normal.
  6. Sand and stain: If the rest of the deck is stained, the new board will be a dramatically different color. For the best blend, wait 2—3 months for the pressure-treated wood to dry (the wood will have a high moisture content when new), then sand lightly and apply the same stain used on the existing deck. For immediate results, use a wood brightener followed by stain after 24 hours.

Tips & Warnings

  • If multiple boards in the same area are damaged, check the underlying joist — moisture from above may have rotted the joist top as well. Probe the joist with a screwdriver for soft spots.
  • Pressure-treated lumber is very wet when new and will shrink as it dries. For areas where appearance matters immediately, look for kiln-dried-after-treatment (KDAT) lumber — it costs more but won't shrink.
  • Use a scrap block on adjacent boards when prying to prevent crowbar dents on the good boards.
  • If hidden fasteners hold the board, you'll need to cut the board out and pull/pry the hidden clip system. Replacement clips are sold separately at most deck supply stores.
