How to Replace a Toilet Flapper

Overview

A worn toilet flapper is the number one cause of a running toilet. The flapper is the rubber valve at the bottom of the tank that opens when you flush and seals to let the tank refill. Over time, the rubber hardens, warps, or picks up mineral deposits, preventing a good seal. Replacement takes 10 minutes and costs $3–$8. If water trickles into the bowl between flushes, the flapper is almost certainly the culprit.

Tools & Materials

  • Toilet flapper (2-inch or 3-inch—measure the old one)
  • Adjustable wrench (if disconnecting the chain clip)
  • Bucket & sponge
  • Rubber gloves
  • Fine sandpaper or Scotch-Brite pad (for the flush valve seat)
  • White vinegar (for mineral deposits)
  • New chain and clip (often included with flapper)

Step-by-Step

  1. Test that the flapper is the problem. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank water. Wait 15 minutes without flushing. If colored water appears in the bowl, the flapper is leaking. If no color, the leak is at the fill valve overflow.
  2. Turn off water and drain the tank. Close the shut-off valve. Flush the toilet. The tank will drain fully since the fill valve cannot refill it. Sponge out any remaining water at the very bottom.
  3. Remove the old flapper. Unhook the chain from the flush handle lever. Pull the flapper's ear holes (side tabs) off the two small pegs on the overflow tube. If there is a ring attachment, slide it off the overflow tube. Some flappers unscrew from a threaded base.
  4. Clean the flush valve seat. The flush valve seat is the smooth plastic ring the flapper sits on at the bottom of the tank. Run your finger around it and remove any grit, mineral buildup, or old flapper rubber residue. A fine Scotch-Brite pad or sandpaper removes mineral deposits. Wipe clean with a rag.
  5. Match the new flapper. Compare the new flapper to the old one. Most are 2-inch, but some modern toilets use a 3-inch flapper for faster flush volume. The flapper must match. Universal flappers come with interchangeable adapters—use the one that fits your flush valve.
  6. Install the new flapper. Hook the side ears onto the overflow tube pegs. If a ring-type, slide it down over the tube. Attach the chain with the clip on the flush lever—there should be about 1/2 inch of slack when the flapper is closed.
  7. Adjust the chain length. Too long: the chain gets caught under the flapper, causing a leak. Too short: the flapper cannot close fully. With the flapper seated, the chain should have just enough slack to hang with a slight U-shape—about 1/2 inch of play.
  8. Test the flush. Turn the water on. Let the tank fill. Flush and watch the operation. The flapper should lift fully, stay up long enough for the flush (about 2–3 seconds), then drop back down and seal completely. Repeat 2–3 times. Listen for any water trickling after the tank is full.

Tips

Tip 1: If the flush valve seat feels pitted or rough after cleaning, you need to replace the entire flush valve assembly—a new flapper will not seal on a damaged seat.
Tip 2: Chlorine tablet in-tank cleaners destroy rubber flappers in 6–12 months. If you use them, expect to replace the flapper annually.
Tip 3: Some flappers are adjustable—they have a dial that controls how much air is trapped inside, which determines how quickly the flapper sinks. Adjust if the flapper drops too fast or stays up too long.
Tip 4: If the chain is too long, do not cut it—just hook the clip a few links higher on the chain. You can always adjust later.
Tip 5: Universally-designed flappers like the Korky 2005BP fit 95% of toilets. Keep a spare on hand—they are the most common replacement part in residential plumbing.
