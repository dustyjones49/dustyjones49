How to Fix a Leaky Faucet

Overview

A drip-a-second faucet wastes over 3,000 gallons of water per year. Most leaks are caused by worn washers, O-rings, or cartridges — all replaceable in under an hour with basic tools. The key is identifying your faucet type: compression (two handles), cartridge (single handle), ball (single handle with a round cap), or ceramic disc (single handle, smooth operation).

Tools & Materials

  • Replacement washer/O-ring/cartridge (take the old part to the hardware store to match)
  • Adjustable wrench, Allen wrench set, pliers, screwdrivers
  • Seat wrench (for compression faucets with worn valve seats)
  • Plumber's grease (silicone-based, not petroleum)
  • Bucket, rags, masking tape (to protect finish), penetrating oil

Step-by-Step

  1. Identify the faucet type: Take a photo from underneath the sink. Compression faucets have separate valves for hot and cold. Cartridge faucets have a cylindrical cartridge under the handle. Ball faucets (common Delta style) have a round metal or plastic cap. Ceramic disc faucets look modern and operate with a quarter-turn.
  2. Shut off water and relieve pressure: Turn off both supply valves under the sink. Open the faucet to drain the water and relieve pressure. Cover the drain with a stopper or rag to prevent small parts from falling in.
  3. Disassemble the faucet: Remove the handle — this may require prying off a decorative cap to access a screw, loosening an Allen set screw, or unscrewing a handle base. Use masking tape on wrench jaws to protect the finish. Unscrew the packing nut and remove the valve stem or cartridge. Lay parts out in order for reassembly.
  4. Replace the worn part: For compression faucets, replace the rubber washer at the bottom of the stem and the O-ring around the stem. For cartridge faucets, swap the entire cartridge. For ball faucets, replace the rubber seats and springs under the ball. For ceramic disc, replace the cylinder assembly. Coat new O-rings and gaskets with plumber's grease.
  5. Inspect and repair the valve seat (compression faucets): Look into the valve body for a rough or grooved metal seat. If the seat is damaged, use a seat-dressing tool (seat grinder) to smooth it, or unscrew the old brass seat with a seat wrench and replace it. A perfect new washer on a rough seat will start leaking again within weeks.
  6. Reassemble and test: Reinstall parts in reverse order. Hand-tighten packing nuts, then turn water back on slowly. Check for leaks around the stem — a small drip means the packing nut needs another 1/8 turn. If the faucet handle is difficult to turn, loosen the packing nut slightly.

Tips & Warnings

  • Always bring the old cartridge or washer to the hardware store. There are hundreds of variations, and it's easy to buy the wrong size by eye.
  • If you have a Moen cartridge stuck in the valve body, buy the Moen cartridge puller tool — it's $20 and saves hours of frustration trying to pry out a seized cartridge.
  • Check the handle screw first before disassembling — sometimes a loose handle screw causes a drip that looks like a washer leak.
  • Use plumber's silicone grease, not petroleum jelly, on O-rings and rubber parts. Petroleum products degrade rubber seals over time.
