How to Pour a Concrete Slab

Overview

Pouring a concrete slab for a patio, shed base, or walkway is a physically demanding project usually tackled with a concrete truck delivery. For slabs over 4x4 feet, order ready-mix concrete delivered rather than mixing bags by hand—a 10x10-foot slab 4 inches thick requires about 1.25 cubic yards (about 68 eighty-pound bags). This guide covers a 10x12 patio slab. Budget 6—8 hours with 2—3 helpers on pour day plus prep over 1—2 weekends. Check whether a permit is required.

Tools & Materials

  • Concrete (order 4—5% extra for waste, 4000 PSI with fiber mesh for crack resistance)
  • 2x4 lumber for forms (straight, no twists)
  • Wooden stakes (2x2, 18-inch minimum)
  • 3-lb sledgehammer, hammer, circular saw
  • 4-foot level, string line, measuring tape
  • Gravel base (4—6-inch base of compacted crushed stone)
  • Welded wire mesh or #4 rebar on 24-inch centers
  • Bull float, magnesium float, edger, groover (concrete finishing tools)
  • Plastic sheeting (for curing) and curing compound
  • Wheelbarrow, shovel, rake, and form ties or snap ties

Step-by-Step

  1. Mark and excavate. Mark the slab outline with stakes and string lines. Check square by measuring diagonals. Excavate 8—10 inches below finish grade—4 inches for the gravel base plus 4 inches for the slab. Slope the excavation away from buildings about 1/4 inch per foot for drainage.
  2. Build and set forms. Using straight 2x4s, build a form at the exact perimeter. The top of the forms determines the finished slab height. Drive stakes every 3 feet along the outside of the forms. Screw forms to stakes. Level the tops of the forms with a carpenter's level or laser level—the forms ARE your finished grade reference.
  3. Install the gravel base. Pour 4—6 inches of crushed aggregate (3/4-inch clear stone or road base). Rake it level. Compact with a plate compactor (rental) in overlapping passes. The gravel base provides drainage and a stable sub-base—do not skip it or the slab will crack from freeze-thaw heaving.
  4. Place reinforcement. Lay welded wire mesh or tie a rebar grid on 24-inch centers. Support the reinforcement on concrete bricks or wire chairs so it sits in the middle of the slab (2 inches up from the base). Overlap mesh sheets by one row of squares and wire-tie together. Rebar crossing points must be wire-tied.
  5. Order and pour the concrete. Schedule delivery for a day with temperatures between 50—70°F and no rain. Direct the chute and spread concrete evenly using rakes and shovels. Fill the forms completely. Tamp along the inside edges with a shovel to remove air pockets and voids. The slab must be poured continuously—cold joints between batches cause cracks.
  6. Screed the surface. Using a straight 2x4 longer than the form width, rest it on the form tops and saw back and forth as you pull it forward. This screeding motion levels the concrete flush with the tops of the forms. Have helpers shovel concrete into low spots and remove excess from high spots.
  7. Bull float immediately. After screeding, run a bull float across the surface with long strokes. This pushes down aggregate and brings cream (cement paste) to the surface. The float handle extends to reach the center without stepping in the concrete.
  8. Wait for bleed water to evaporate. After bull floating, stop. Wait until the surface water (bleed water) evaporates and the concrete is firm enough that a footprint leaves about a 1/4-inch indentation. This may take 1—3 hours depending on temperature. Do not work water back into the surface—it weakens the top layer.
  9. Edge, groove, and trowel. Run an edger along the forms to round the edges (prevents chipping). Cut control joints with a groover every 8—10 feet in both directions (joints should be 1/4 the slab depth). Magnesium float the surface to a smooth finish. For a broom finish (non-slip), drag a stiff push broom across the surface after the final trowel pass.
  10. Cure the slab. Cover the slab with plastic sheeting weighted at the edges, or spray with curing compound. Concrete cures chemically with water—it must stay damp for 7 days to reach full strength. Mist the slab daily if not covered. Wait 24 hours before light foot traffic and 7 days before vehicle traffic.

Tips

Tip 1: Call 811 before digging. Utility locating is free and required by law in every state. Hitting a gas or power line can be fatal.
Tip 2: Concrete trucks need direct access. If the truck cannot reach the pour site, you will need a concrete pump ($300 additional) or you will be wheelbarrowing—order a smaller load in that case.
Tip 3: Control joints (grooves) do not prevent concrete from cracking—they direct the inevitable crack beneath the groove, where it is invisible. Skip them at your own peril.
Tip 4: Pouring in hot weather (above 85°F) causes rapid water evaporation and surface cracking. If you must pour in heat, use a set-retarding admixture and keep the surface misted during finishing.
Tip 5: Have all tools ready and helpers briefed before the concrete truck arrives. Once the pour starts, you have about 90 minutes to place and screed before the concrete starts setting.
