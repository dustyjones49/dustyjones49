How to Replace a Toilet Fill Valve

Overview

The fill valve is the mechanism on the left side of the tank that refills the toilet after each flush. When it fails, the toilet either runs constantly, fills slowly, or makes a loud hissing noise. Replacement is a 30-minute job requiring no special skills. Universal fill valves fit virtually every toilet and cost $10–$20. Replace the supply line at the same time if it is old or rigid.

Tools & Materials

  • Universal toilet fill valve (Fluidmaster 400A or similar)
  • Adjustable wrench & channel-lock pliers
  • Flathead screwdriver
  • Bucket & sponge or towel
  • New flexible braided stainless steel supply line (optional, recommended)
  • Plumber's tape (Teflon)
  • Rubber gloves

Step-by-Step

  1. Turn off the water. Locate the shut-off valve behind the toilet (usually an oval or lever handle on the wall or floor). Turn clockwise until it stops. Flush the toilet to drain most of the water from the tank. Sponge out any remaining water in the bottom of the tank.
  2. Disconnect the supply line. Place a bucket under the shut-off valve. Unscrew the supply line nut where it connects to the fill valve at the bottom of the tank. Use pliers if needed but be careful not to bend the thin tank wall. Let remaining water drain into the bucket.
  3. Remove the old fill valve. Under the tank, unscrew the large plastic locknut that holds the old fill valve to the tank. This nut is often hand-tight but may need pliers. Once the nut is off, lift the old fill valve straight up and out of the tank.
  4. Adjust the new fill valve height. The Fluidmaster-style valve has a twist-lock adjustment. Set the valve body so the top of the threaded shank is the correct height—typically the top of the valve (C.L. mark) should be at least 1 inch above the overflow tube. Then lock it in place.
  5. Install the new fill valve. Insert the threaded shank through the hole in the bottom of the tank. From below, thread the plastic locknut onto the shank and hand-tighten, then give it a quarter-turn with pliers. Do not overtighten—the plastic nut can crack the tank or strip the threads.
  6. Connect the fill hose. Attach the small rubber refill hose to the top of the fill valve nipple and clip the other end into the overflow tube. The hose must point down into the overflow tube—do not just drop it in where it might fall out.
  7. Reconnect the supply line. Wrap the fill valve threads with 2–3 wraps of Teflon tape. Screw the supply line nut onto the fill valve shank and tighten with pliers—snug, not crushed. If replacing the supply line, attach the other end to the shut-off valve the same way.
  8. Turn on water and adjust. Open the shut-off valve fully. The tank should fill. Adjust the water level by pinching the spring clip on the float rod and sliding the float up or down. The water should stop about 1/2 inch below the top of the overflow tube. Test flush and fine-tune the level as needed.

Tips

Tip 1: Before replacing the fill valve, try cleaning the old one. Dirt stuck in the valve is often the only problem. Pop off the cap, rinse the rubber diaphragm, and reassemble.
Tip 2: Replace the flexible supply line with a braided stainless steel version at the same time. Old plastic or rigid chrome lines are prone to cracking and cost $6 to replace.
Tip 3: If the toilet still runs after replacing the fill valve, the flapper is likely the problem—not the fill valve. Replace the flapper ($5) before changing the fill valve again.
Tip 4: Do not overtighten the plastic locknut on the fill valve. If you hear cracking sounds or the tank bottom deforms, stop immediately.
Tip 5: When you turn the water back on, water may briefly spray from the fill valve cap. This is normal and stops within seconds as the diaphragm seats.
