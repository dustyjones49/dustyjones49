How to Replace an Electrical Outlet

Overview

Replacing a worn or outdated electrical outlet is a basic electrical project that takes about 15 minutes per receptacle. The most common reasons to replace: loose plugs that fall out, cracked face plates, upgrading to tamper-resistant (TR) receptacles (required by NEC in new construction), or adding a GFCI where required. This is a safe DIY task if you follow lockout-tagout procedures.

Tools & Materials

  • New receptacle (15A or 20A, standard or GFCI, TR-rated for residential)
  • Non-contact voltage tester (must-have safety tool)
  • Flathead and Phillips screwdrivers, needle-nose pliers, wire strippers
  • New wall plate (if your old one is cracked or won't fit the new outlet)
  • Electrical tape (optional, for wrapping outlet terminals)

Step-by-Step

  1. Turn off the power and verify: Turn off the circuit breaker for the outlet. Use a non-contact voltage tester on the outlet — test both sockets (top and bottom — they may be on different circuits in a switched configuration). Insert the tester into each slot and confirm no beeping. Test the tester on a known live circuit first to confirm it works.
  2. Remove the old outlet: Remove the cover plate, then remove the two mounting screws holding the outlet to the box. Gently pull the outlet out of the box. Take a photo of the wire connections before touching anything — this is your reference if you get confused.
  3. Identify wires and labeling: Look at the old outlet. The brass screws (or "HOT" side) hold the black wire, the silver screws (or "NEUTRAL" side) hold the white wire, and the green screw holds the bare copper or green wire. Disconnect the wires by loosening the terminal screws, or cut the wires and strip fresh ends if the old wires are nicked or damaged.
  4. Prepare the wires: If you cut the old wires, strip about 3/4" of insulation from each. Create a hook at the end of each wire using the needle-nose pliers — the hook should fully wrap around the screw terminal in a clockwise direction (so tightening the screw pulls the wire tighter, not looser).
  5. Connect the new outlet: Hook black wire around the brass screw, white wire around the silver screw, and bare/green wire around the green ground screw. Tighten each screw firmly. If your receptacle has back-wire clamp terminals (approved type), strip wires straight and insert into the rear terminals, tightening the clamping screws. Do not use push-in "stab" connections on the back — these are unreliable over time.
  6. Install GFCI (if needed): If replacing with a GFCI, identify which pair of wires is the LINE side (incoming power from the panel) and which is the LOAD side (feeding downstream outlets). Connect the LINE black and white wires to the LINE brass and silver terminals. Connect the LOAD wires to the LOAD terminals if you want downstream GFCI protection. All GFCIs include a label sheet — apply the "GFCI Protected" sticker to all downstream outlets.
  7. Fold wires and mount: Carefully fold the wires accordion-style into the box, with the ground wire at the back. Mount the outlet with the two screws at top and bottom. Do not over-tighten — the outlet should be secure but not flexing. Attach the cover plate. Turn the breaker on and test with the voltage tester and a plug-in lamp.

Tips & Warnings

  • If the receptacle has four wires rather than two (two blacks on brass, two whites on silver), one set is feeding through to downstream outlets. Keep the pairs together on the new outlet, or use a pigtail: connect a short jumper wire to the outlet terminal, then wire-nut the jumper to both black wires.
  • Never install a 20A outlet on a 15A circuit. A 15A outlet on a 20A circuit is acceptable (NEC) because a 20A plug won't fit in it, preventing overcurrent on the receptacle.
  • If the electrical box is metal and ungrounded (no bare wire coming into the box from the panel), you cannot safely install a standard grounded outlet. Install a GFCI marked "No Equipment Ground" instead.
  • Wrap the outlet's terminal screws with a single layer of electrical tape after wiring to prevent accidental contact with the metal box when pushing the outlet in.
