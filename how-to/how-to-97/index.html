How to Prune Trees and Shrubs

Overview

Pruning improves plant health, controls size, encourages flowers and fruit, and removes hazardous branches. The key rules: prune at the right time of year for the species, use sharp bypass tools (not anvil), and never remove more than 25–30% of a plant's live canopy in one season.

Tools & Materials

  • Bypass hand pruners (for branches up to 3/4")
  • Bypass loppers (for branches 3/4" to 1-1/2")
  • Pruning saw (for branches over 1-1/2")
  • Pole saw (for high branches)
  • Rubbing alcohol or 10% bleach solution (for tool disinfection)
  • Work gloves
  • Safety glasses
  • Hard hat (for overhead work)

Step-by-Step

  1. Prune at the right time. Late winter/early spring (while dormant) for most deciduous trees and shrubs. Spring-flowering shrubs (lilac, forsythia, azalea): prune immediately after they finish blooming. Summer-flowering shrubs (rose of Sharon, butterfly bush): prune in late winter. Dead or damaged branches: remove any time.
  2. Remove the 3 D's first. Start with Dead, Diseased, and Damaged branches. Cut back to healthy wood, just above a bud or branch junction. Disinfect tools with alcohol between cuts when working on diseased plants.
  3. Remove crossing and rubbing branches. Branches that cross and rub create wounds that invite pests and disease. Remove the weaker or less desirable of the two crossing branches.
  4. Remove suckers and water sprouts. Suckers grow from the base of the tree or from rootstock below a graft. Water sprouts are thin, vertical shoots growing straight up from the trunk or main branches. Both sap energy and should be removed.
  5. Make proper heading cuts. When shortening a branch, cut 1/4" above a bud that faces the direction you want new growth to go (usually outward). Cut at a 45-degree angle, sloping away from the bud. Too close kills the bud; too far leaves a stub that rots.
  6. Make proper thinning cuts. When removing an entire branch, cut just outside the branch collar (the swollen area where the branch meets the trunk or larger branch). The collar contains healing tissue. Don't leave a stub and don't cut flush with the trunk.
  7. Use the 3-cut method for large branches. For branches over 1" diameter: Cut 1: Underside cut 6–12" out from the trunk, 1/3 of the way through. Cut 2: Top cut 1–2" further out to remove the branch, letting it fall. Cut 3: Remove the remaining stub at the branch collar. This prevents bark from tearing down the trunk.
  8. Thin for light and air. For shrubs, selectively remove up to 1/3 of the oldest, thickest stems at ground level each year. This opens the center for sunlight and air circulation, encouraging fresh growth from the base.
  9. Step back and assess. After every few cuts, step back and observe the overall shape. It's easy to get tunnel vision and over-prune. You can always cut more, but you can't put branches back.

Pro Tips

Tip 1: Don't top trees. Cutting back the main leader or topping large branches creates weak, bushy regrowth and large wounds that never heal properly. It also makes trees look awful.
Tip 2: Never use pruning sealant (wound dressing) on cuts. Research shows it traps moisture and inhibits natural healing. Let cuts air-dry and heal naturally.
Tip 3: Disinfect tools between plants—not just between cuts on the same plant. Diseases like fire blight spread easily on pruning tools.
Tip 4: For branches near power lines, stop. Call the utility company or a certified arborist. Contact with live wires is instantly fatal.
