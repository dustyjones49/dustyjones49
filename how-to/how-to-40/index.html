How to Clean a P-Trap

Overview

The P-trap is the U-shaped pipe under every sink. Its job is to hold water to block sewer gas while allowing waste to pass through. Over time, it collects hair, soap scum, grease, and debris that slow drainage and cause odors. Cleaning it takes 15 minutes and requires no special plumbing skills. If a sink drains slowly but still drains, the P-trap blockage is likely the cause—clear it before snaking the deeper line.

Tools & Materials

  • Adjustable wrench or channel-lock pliers
  • Bucket (positioned directly under the trap)
  • Rubber gloves (the contents will be unpleasant)
  • Wire coat hanger or bottle brush
  • Old toothbrush or small scrub brush
  • Replacement slip-joint washers (1¼-inch or 1½-inch)
  • Plumber's tape (Teflon)
  • Flashlight

Step-by-Step

  1. Place a bucket under the trap. Position it directly beneath the P-trap. The water in the trap WILL spill out—about 1–2 cups. Lay a towel on the cabinet floor for drips.
  2. Loosen the slip nuts. The P-trap is held by two slip nuts—one at the tailpiece from the sink, one at the wall drainpipe. Loosen both by hand if possible. If they are tight, use pliers—but cushion the jaws with a rag to avoid scarring the plastic nuts. Turn counterclockwise.
  3. Remove the P-trap. Once both nuts are loose, pull the trap straight down. Let the water drain into the bucket. Be prepared for a foul smell—the water in the trap is stagnant waste water. Do not drop the rubber washers that will fall out of the slip joints.
  4. Clean the P-trap. Take the trap to a utility sink or outside. Shake out loose debris first. Push a straightened wire coat hanger or bottle brush through the trap to dislodge compacted sludge. Scrub the interior with an old toothbrush and hot soapy water. Rinse thoroughly.
  5. Inspect the tailpiece and wall pipe. While the trap is off, shine a flashlight into both pipes. The tailpiece (coming from the sink) may have a buildup ring at the top that you can scrape with a screwdriver. The wall pipe may have debris just inside that you can scoop with a bent wire.
  6. Replace the slip-joint washers. Examine the clear or rubber washers: if they are cracked, flattened, or misshapen, replace them. These cost pennies and prevent leaks after reinstalling. A fresh washer seats better and eliminates that slow drip you could never fully stop.
  7. Reinstall the P-trap. Slide a slip nut and washer onto each pipe end. Position the trap and hand-tighten both nuts. Do not cross-thread—plumbing nuts are easy to cross-thread, especially plastic ones. Once hand-tight, give each nut a quarter-turn with pliers.
  8. Test for leaks. Run water for 30 seconds. Check both slip-nut connections with a dry paper towel. Even a slow seep will show on a dry towel. If leaking, tighten another eighth-turn. If still leaking, disassemble and check that the washer is seated correctly—not pinched or crooked.

Tips

Tip 1: Plumber's tape on slip-joint threads usually does not help—the seal is made by the washer compressing against the pipe, not by the threads. Over-taping threads can cause the nut to crack.
Tip 2: If the slip nuts are completely frozen (metal on metal), do not force them. Cut them off with a hacksaw or oscillating tool and replace with new nuts and washers.
Tip 3: In the bathroom, the P-trap will be clogged with hair and soap (a gray-brown paste). In the kitchen, it will be grease and food particles. Bathroom clogs are the most foul-smelling.
Tip 4: After cleaning, pour a pot of boiling water mixed with a tablespoon of dish soap slowly down the drain. This helps flush loosened debris past the trap through the rest of the drain.
Tip 5: If you have an S-trap (no vent, curved like an S) instead of a P-trap, it is likely a code violation and should be replaced with a proper P-trap and air admittance valve.
