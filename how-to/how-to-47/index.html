How to Build Deck Stairs

Overview

Building deck stairs requires precise math and careful cutting, but the process is formulaic once you understand rise and run. Stair code typically requires a maximum rise of 7-3/4 inches and minimum run of 10 inches (with tread nose). The total rise is the vertical distance from the ground to the deck surface. The number of risers equals total rise divided by 7-1/2 (aim for this target). Budget a full day for a 4-step set of stairs.

Tools & Materials

  • Pressure-treated 2x12 lumber (stringers—one for every 16 inches of stair width)
  • Pressure-treated 2x6 or 5/4 decking (tread material)
  • Framing square with stair gauges (brass buttons that clamp to the square)
  • Circular saw (finish cuts with a handsaw—do not over-cut stringers)
  • Miter saw, drill/driver, structural screws
  • 4-foot level, tape measure, pencil
  • Concrete stair pads or gravel base for landing
  • Galvanized stringer hangers or lag bolts for ledger flanges

Step-by-Step

  1. Measure the total rise. Measure from the top of the deck (finished surface) straight down to the ground or landing point. Use a level and straight board if measuring from above is easier. This is your total rise—the number everything else is calculated from.
  2. Calculate risers and treads. Divide the total rise by 7-1/2. Round to the nearest whole number—this is the number of risers. Divide the total rise by the number of risers to get the exact riser height (must be under 7-3/4 inches). The run is typically 10-1/2 inches for 2x12 treads (two 5-1/2-inch boards with a 1/2-inch gap).
  3. Example calculation: Total rise = 32 inches. 32 / 7.5 = 4.27. Round to 4 risers. Exact riser = 32 / 4 = 8.0 inches. That is over 7-3/4, so use 5 risers. Exact riser = 32 / 5 = 6.4 inches—perfect.
  4. Mark the stringer layout. Attach stair gauges to a framing square at the exact riser and run dimensions. Place the square on the edge of a 2x12 and trace the rise-and-run triangle. Slide the square down and repeat, tracing each step. Mark a level cut at the top and a plumb cut at the bottom.
  5. Cut the stringers. Cut along all layout lines with a circular saw. Stop at the inside corner of each cut—do not overcut, as this creates a weak point where the stringer will crack. Finish the inside corner cuts with a handsaw. Use the first stringer as a template to mark and cut the remaining stringers.
  6. Attach stringers to the deck. For attachment to the deck, use galvanized stringer hangers bolted to the rim joist or block inside the deck rim and lag the stringer to it. The top of the stringer must be flush with or slightly below the top of the deck surface—never proud, or it creates a trip hazard.
  7. Set the bottom landing. Pour a concrete pad, set precast stair landing pads, or dig and fill a gravel base. The bottom of the stringers must rest on a solid, level surface—not dirt, which settles. Treated wood placed in the dirt rots within 5 years.
  8. Install the treads. Screw two 2x6 boards per tread (or use one 2x12 if code allows) with a 1/4-inch gap between them for drainage. Each board gets two screws per stringer. Overhang the treads about 3/4 inch past the stringer sides and 1 inch (nose) past the riser. Install riser boards next if using closed risers.
  9. Install handrails. Code requires a graspable handrail on at least one side of stairs with 4 or more risers. Mount handrail brackets to stair stringers or posts. The handrail must be 34—38 inches above the stair nosing and return to the wall or end in a smooth turn.

Tips

Tip 1: The bottom riser height can be up to 3/8 inch different from the others without being a trip hazard. Adjust by cutting the bottom of the stringers if the ground is uneven.
Tip 2: Stair gauges ($4) clamp to a framing square and make marking repeated rise-run steps foolproof. Do not try to freehand layout—a 1/8-inch error compounds across every step.
Tip 3: The throat of the stringer (the narrowest point of the cut) must be at least 3-1/2 inches for structural strength. For stairs wider than 36 inches, add a middle stringer.
Tip 4: Use the thickest 2x12 you can find for stringers. Look for boards with no large knots near the edges where you will make the deepest cuts.
Tip 5: Pre-drill screw holes near the ends of tread boards to prevent splitting. Treated lumber is much more prone to splitting than untreated wood.
