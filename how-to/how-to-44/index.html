How to Winterize Outdoor Faucets

Overview

Winterizing outdoor faucets prevents freeze damage that bursts pipes inside the wall, causing thousands of dollars in water damage. The process takes 15 minutes per faucet and must be done before the first hard freeze (below 28°F overnight). There are two types: frost-free sillcocks (the valve seat is 8–16 inches inside the warm wall) and standard hose bibs. Even frost-free faucets need winterizing—the hose must be disconnected or the trapped water freezes back into the wall.

Tools & Materials

  • Foam outdoor faucet cover (insulated dome that straps on)
  • Flathead screwdriver
  • Bucket & towels
  • Adjustable wrench
  • Plumber's tape (Teflon)
  • Replacement rubber washer (if the faucet drips)
  • Pipe insulation (for interior exposed pipe near the wall)

Step-by-Step

  1. Disconnect all hoses. Unscrew garden hoses, timers, and splitters from every outdoor faucet. This is the most critical step—a connected hose traps water that freezes back into the faucet and pipe inside the house. Drain the hose and store it indoors or in a shed.
  2. Shut off the interior supply valve. Most homes have a separate shut-off valve for each outdoor faucet, located in the basement or crawl space where the pipe passes through the wall. Turn this valve clockwise to close. If your home does not have interior shut-off valves, skip to step 4.
  3. Drain the small bleeder cap. After closing the shut-off valve, look for a small brass cap on the side of the valve. Place a bucket under it, unscrew the cap with a screwdriver, and let the water between the valve and the faucet drain out completely. Replace the cap finger-tight.
  4. Open the outdoor faucet fully. Leave the outdoor handle in the full OPEN position (turned all the way on) for the entire winter. This allows any remaining water that might freeze to expand out the open end rather than splitting the pipe inside the wall.
  5. Install a foam covers. Place an insulated foam faucet cover over the outdoor spigot. Tighten the strap or drawstring so the cover is snug against the wall. This provides R-value insulation for the pipe inside the wall and blocks wind chill through the wall penetration.
  6. Insulate exposed interior pipes. In the basement or crawl space, wrap any exposed copper pipe leading to the faucet with foam pipe insulation, especially near the foundation wall where it is coldest. If the rim joist area is drafty, seal air leaks with spray foam around the pipe penetration.
  7. Check for drips before winter. If the outdoor faucet drips even when fully closed, repair the rubber washer or replace the faucet before winter. A dripping faucet has water constantly in the pipe near the cold wall, exponentially increasing the freeze risk.
  8. In spring: reverse the process. In spring, close the outdoor faucet handle, remove the foam cover, open the interior shut-off valve, and check for any leaks that might have developed over winter before reconnecting the hose.

Tips

Tip 1: A foam faucet cover costs $3 and can prevent a $5,000 pipe burst repair. Install them on every outdoor faucet, including ones you never use.
Tip 2: Frost-free sillcocks are only frost-free if the hose is disconnected. The long valve stem keeps the seal deep inside the warm wall, but a connected hose fills the entire pipe with water.
Tip 3: If you do not have interior shut-off valves, leave the outdoor faucet in the full open position all winter with a cover installed. The open valve provides an expansion path.
Tip 4: For vacation homes or buildings left unheated over winter, drain the entire plumbing system and pour RV antifreeze into all drain traps. Outdoor faucets alone are not the only freeze risk.
Tip 5: In extreme cold climates (below -20°F), standard covers may not be enough. Add a towel or fiberglass insulation inside the foam cover for extra R-value.
